# Chapter 6 — Emotion

> Abridged from Sergio Paez & Anson Jew, *Professional Storyboarding: Rules of Thumb* (Focal Press, 2013), Ch. 6.
> **Scope:** why emotion is the purpose behind every shot decision, how to make feeling *visual* rather than stated, and how juxtaposition (the Kuleshov effect) manufactures emotion "in the cut".

## Core idea

Emotion is the foundation for every action in a story — audiences pay to *feel*, even to suffer feelings they would avoid in life. Get the "why" (the emotional driving force) right and the "how" follows easily; without meaning attached to the outcome, even a spectacular battle is boring. Meaning has to be *built up* in the protagonist over the story so that, when the object of desire is near, the emotional payoff lands with weight. And crucially, **emotion trumps logic**: when a technical problem (screen direction, character placement) collides with the emotional beat, serve the beat. You can carry small logic holes if you don't talk down to the audience — what you must never do is let the audience *sense the manipulation*. (Note the diminishing return: a repeated emotional experience lands at half power the second time.)

The board artist's specific power is to **make the mental physical** — "show, don't tell". Ask what the character is thinking, whether it matches what they say, and what the subtext is; then invent a *visual* cue for it. The lazy default is a facial expression; the craft is a pose or action that externalises the feeling — a man shifts his tie, or beats eggs too hard, and we read the anger without a line of dialogue.

## Juxtaposition of shots — telling the story "in the cut"

Emotion is easy to solicit because the audience *wants* to supply it. Hold on an image long enough and viewers project feeling onto it; hold a waving flag and they conjure patriotism or nostalgia from their own experience — then **cut to a face, and they attribute that feeling to the character**. This is the **Kuleshov effect**: the same neutral shot of an actor's face, inter-cut with soup, a girl, or a child's coffin, read to audiences as hunger, desire, and grief — though the face never changed. Eisenstein pushed the same idea as the "collision" of shots to control feeling and build visual metaphor.

The transferable lesson: emotion is generated by the *sequence*, not the performance. So the sophisticated way to show sadness is not to film someone crying — it is to juxtapose a man's burning house with his face looking at it. No dialogue, no acting required; the viewer's own reaction becomes the character's. This is the artist's greatest weapon: even with flat dialogue, the *choice and ordering* of shots can turn a dull exchange into a rich emotional scene.

## Studio application

- **Emotion is the intent that biases every default — the "why" behind a heuristic's pick.** It is not a separate axis to encode; it is the global style contract that tilts which `ShotSize`, `CameraAngle`, `FocalLength` or lighting a role reaches for in [camera.py](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py) and the crew's `heuristic` methods in [role.py](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py). Dread biases toward low angles and long lenses; sympathy toward closer, frontal, eye-level framing. The intent chooses; the grammar merely realises.
- **The Kuleshov effect is the edit layer's engine — meaning is a property of the *sequence*, not the panel.** A single [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py) / [`ImageStudio`](../../../sequitur/image.py) keyframe is emotionally neutral until placed next to its neighbour; the feeling is manufactured when the [Editor](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py) orders shots into a [`Sequence`](../../../sequitur/edit.py). This is why the board is the first editorial pass — see [Grammar of the Edit Ch. 5 — When to Cut](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md).
- **"Emotion trumps logic" is the priority rule when contracts conflict.** If a continuity constraint (screen direction, the 180° line from Ch. 4) fights the emotional beat, the beat wins — the same subordination the [Director](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) enforces over lower-level role defaults.
- **Overlaps the tone/style sources — this is the affective "why" they set as a project-wide contract.** Cross-reference [Directing Ch. 10 — Form and Style](../../directing/reference/ch10-form-and-style.md) and [Ch. 11 — Tone, Style, and Genre](../../directing/reference/ch11-tone-style-and-genre.md), and the narrating attitude in [The Screenwriter's Taxonomy Ch. 5 — Voice](../../the%20screenwriter's%20taxonomy/reference/ch05-voice.md). Those establish the feeling the studio is contracted to deliver; "make the mental physical" is how a board turns that contract into concrete framing.
