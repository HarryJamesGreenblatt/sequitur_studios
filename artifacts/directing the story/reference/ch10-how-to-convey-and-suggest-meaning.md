# Chapter 10 — How to Convey and Suggest Meaning

> Abridged from Francis Glebas, *Directing the Story: Professional Storytelling and Storyboarding Techniques for Live Action and Animation* (Routledge/Focal Press), Ch. 10.
> **Scope:** how juxtaposed images make the audience *construct* meaning — continuity, the many kinds of causality, screen geography, retroactive anchoring, pacing, and the motivated cut — the machinery by which a sequence of shots becomes a felt story.

## Core idea

The audience is not a passive receiver; it is an active meaning-maker the director *steers*. Film's one unique power is **juxtaposition** — cutting a continuous event into pieces and reassembling them so the viewer's mind stitches the gaps back into a seamless world. Give the audience *enough* clues to build the story but don't spell everything out: withholding recruits their imagination, and a story they help construct is a story they feel is their own. Every shot is a close view of *exactly* what you want them to see, and every cut is a deliberate act that (a) directs attention, (b) intensifies emotion by closing distance, and (c) surprises, because the viewer can't look ahead. Meaning is the payload; the cut is the delivery mechanism.

## Continuity: the invisible art

**Classical continuity** makes the film's assembly invisible — no visible gap in space, no break in the temporal flow. Editing is "the invisible art" because a good cut sits *below the viewer's threshold of awareness*; a mishandled one (a glitch, a jump, a crossed line) surfaces above that threshold and breaks the spell. The whole apparatus — structure, staging, cutting — works to keep the illusion seamless so the viewer stays *lost in the story*.

A "glitch" is where actions fail to hook up: the causal chain skips a link the audience needed. The remedy is coverage — shoot more than you need, overlap the actions, and choose in the edit where to join them.

## The kinds of causality

Causality is the glue that welds juxtaposed shots into a single world; **narrative questions** ("what happens next?") are the drive that keeps us watching. But physical cause-and-effect is only one kind, and the predictable kind. Glebas' taxonomy of causes is a directing tool — pick the causality that will *earn* the viewer's next question:

| Causality | The cause is… | Directing use |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | a physical force (Rube Goldberg, Newton) | action, slapstick; predictable, so leaven with Murphy's Law |
| **Linguistic** | a word ("Please continue reading") | speech acts — a command or promise sets an expectation |
| **Emotional** | a feeling or a look ("if looks could kill") | character motivation — the key to believable characters |
| **Semiotic** | a sign we respond to (a red light, "one if by land") | props, symbols, signals that *mean* and therefore *cause* |
| **Supernatural** | no explainable reason at all | horror, fate; the effect is built in the *viewer's* mind as they make sense of the edit |

Two structural facts fall out of this. First, **mere juxtaposition implies cause**: press a button in shot one, a car explodes in shot two, and the mind welds them — even if the button was only a doorbell. Second, **motivation is emotional causality made visible**: characters act *because* they feel and want, and it is those "why"s the audience tracks. A movie is a signifying-systems machine — a great chain of causes and effects arranged inside a hierarchy of narrative questions, simplified so the audience can read a life-lesson out of it.

## Screen geography: where are we?

Viewers build a mental map from the screen, and action is judged by how it *appears*, not how it was staged. Keep them oriented:

- **Open on a stage view** — a wide that shows everything in relation to everything else.
- **Hold the line of action** — run an imaginary line through the action and stay on one side, or a character will appear to reverse direction across a cut (west = screen-left by map convention).
- **Cover a direction change honestly** — insert a *neutral shot* (head-on camera axis) or show the turn in a longer shot; a reaction shot can also bridge it.
- **Match eyelines** — characters in separate shots must align their looks to appear to be looking at each other; a look is normally followed by a shot of what they see.
- **Time entrances and exits** — doors call attention to themselves; time them so they don't step on a more important beat.

## Time is always *now* — and always constructed

Film reads as a continuously flowing present; past and future exist only when *framed* by context ("I remember back in 1971 …"). Four clocks run at once, and only the last two are the director's real material:

- **Chronological time** — the running time of the film.
- **Story time** — the elapsed time of the events (hours or years).
- **Constructed time** — the imaginary timeline the viewer assembles.
- **Emotional time** — how long the film *felt*.

## Pacing: the expansion and contraction of time

Pacing balances the film between **boredom and confusion**. Too fast or too dense and the audience can't process the story and so can't feel it; too slow or too thin and they drift. So the director **contracts** time to move the story along (cut the "passage work," the boring parts with no narrative question) and **expands** it to milk a dramatic moment — extra significant detail buys the audience time to feel. Jump cuts hide inside the dramatic flow: start an action, cut away, return with it further along, and nothing feels missed. The **ticking clock** drives a climax against a deadline — but an emotionally paced clock deliberately runs *longer* than its stated time so the audience can drink in the peak (a good climax stretches; only a bad film's clock keeps real time).

## Why cuts work

Walter Murch's rule (*In the Blink of an Eye*): the ideal cut satisfies six criteria at once — **emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, the 2-D plane of the screen, and the 3-D space of the action** — but in that priority order. If the **emotion is right and the story advances**, the audience forgives violations of the other four. *The audience follows the story, not individual actions.* Cuts feel natural because juxtaposition mirrors how we think and speak — one thing at a time.

## Two anchoring principles

**Retroactive meaning.** We watch stories forward but make sense of them backwards. Meaning in a chain of images (like a sentence) is not fixed shot-by-shot; it is anchored *at the end*, by the final "punctuation." A single reveal can rewrite everything already seen (*The Sixth Sense*, *Fight Club*, *The Village*). Corollary: full meaning is delayed until the film is over — so **storytelling is story-delaying**.

**The Kuleshov effect.** Juxtaposition gives a shot meaning it did not itself contain, and *order* determines that meaning. Neutral face → gun → terrified face reads as fear; reverse the two face-shots and the same footage reads as courage. The whole is greater than the sum of the parts — the audience builds the emotion the cut only implies.

## The rule of threes and the motivated cut

**Rule of threes** — one or two events show no pattern; three establish one, which the director can then confirm or *break*. Try / fail, try / fail, try / **succeed** raises stakes (the pattern predicts a third failure) and buys surprise when the third attempt wins.

**Choose which part of the action to show** — anticipation, the action itself, or the aftermath. Each part suggests the whole:

- Show only the **anticipation** → the audience wonders whether it happened.
- Show only the **aftermath** → it implies the event already occurred off-screen.
- Show lead-up, cut, then return after → it happened while we were away.

The more you make the audience *construct* the missing piece, the more they own the moment and the more they feel.

**Cut for a reason.** For a cut to stay invisible it must be *motivated* — above all, to move the story forward. Other valid motives: delay a narrative question; give a new view or new information; set rhythm; supply context with a wider view; connect or contrast two shots; recall an earlier scene; show what a character is looking at; point out a detail; or compress time by cutting the boring parts.

## Studio application

- **This chapter is the Editor's charter — every entry in the "motivated cut" list is an [`EditReason`](../../../sequitur/edit.py) waiting to be enumerated.** Glebas' reasons-to-cut map almost one-to-one onto the Editor's owned vocabulary in [`crew/editorial.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py) (`Transition` / `EditReason` / `EditCategory`); the discipline "cut for a reason, or the cut surfaces above the threshold" is exactly the constraint the Editor persona reasons under, reconciled the same way as [Grammar of the Edit Ch. 5](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md) and [Ch. 6](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch06-transitions-and-edit-categories.md).
- **The taxonomy of causality is a [`Screenwriter`](../../../sequitur/crew/screenwriting.py) treatment discipline, not a shot enum.** Physical / linguistic / emotional / semiotic / supernatural causality names *why the next beat follows* — the emotional spine a treatment must make explicit before the crew renders a frame. Emotional causality (= character motivation) is the load-bearing kind; it is what a heuristic reconciler can't invent and a Director `PersonaJudgment` (the "B" tier) must supply ([`judgment.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py)).
- **Screen geography is a hard cross-department constraint the Director reconciles into a [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py).** The line of action, eyeline matches, and neutral direction-covering shots gate the DP's coverage and the Editor's cut order; the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) enforces spatial continuity across a sequence the same way it enforces POV — a crossed line is a rendered-continuity glitch a per-shot Omni render can produce and the Director must catch.
- **"Storytelling is story-delaying" + retroactive meaning is the dailies-gate argument.** Because full meaning anchors only at the end, no single [rendered still](../../../sequitur/image.py) or [clip](../../../sequitur/studio.py) is self-justifying — its value depends on the withholding schedule around it. That is why the Producer reviews *sequences* at a phase gate, not frames in isolation ([architecture.md](../../../context/architecture.md)).

> **Overlap flag:** Glebas Ch. 10 (building meaning from juxtaposed images) is the story-artist's face of **[Directing (Rabiger) Ch. 10 — Form and Style](../../directing/reference/ch10-form-and-style.md)**. Rabiger frames form/style as the director's controlling aesthetic; Glebas gives the shot-to-shot *mechanics* of how that form actually generates meaning in the viewer's head. Reconcile them as one idea: form is the grammar, causality-plus-cutting is how the grammar *means*.
