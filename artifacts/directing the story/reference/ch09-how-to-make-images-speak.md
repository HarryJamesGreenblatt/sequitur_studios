# Chapter 9 — How to Make Images Speak: The Hidden Power of Images

> Abridged from Francis Glebas, *Directing the Story: Professional Storytelling and Storyboarding Techniques for Live Action and Animation* (Routledge/Focal Press), Ch. 9.
> **Scope:** the semiotic craft that turns a legible image into a *meaning-bearing* one — denotation vs. connotation, the icon/index/symbol sign types, significant objects, the four master tropes (metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony), speaking indirectly across time, and reading genre "codes."

## Core idea

Once you can direct the eye and make an image *read*, the next power is making an image **speak** — carry a story point without words. Glebas frames this through **semiotics**, "a fancy word for clues." A picture is never just its content: it is filled with signs the audience actively reads to construct the story for themselves. Because minds work by **association** (immediate) far more than by logic (effortful) — and stories persuade by evoking associations — the director's job is to plant the right signs, *stack* their meanings, and **point at the clues so the audience doesn't miss them**. This chapter is the argument that a shot must *carry* a story point, and the toolkit for loading it.

## Denotation and connotation

- **Denotation** — the literal, dictionary meaning. A drawing of a bundle of sticks *denotes* "a bundle of sticks" (the fact that it's a drawing doesn't change the denotation).
- **Connotation** — the associations it evokes: warmth, strength-in-numbers, unity, togetherness. Some connotations are **shared** (everyone knows fire's warmth); some are private "roots" the filmmaker plants and can later recall.
- **Connotations stack.** *The Straight Story*'s closing bundle of sticks reads as: sticks (denotation) → strength/warmth/unity → the good of *family* → a *gift* → "thank you" → a signifier of the film itself. Present one layer and it triggers the whole stack. Contrast is a loader too: Donald alone *beside* animals paired two-by-two reads as *even more* alone.

## Sign, signifier, signified

The sign splits into two parts (semiotics' "splitting of the atom"):

- **Signifier** — the physical support: what we see or hear (image, phoneme).
- **Signified** — the concept it raises in the mind.

There are three sign types — the working taxonomy:

| **Sign type** | **Relationship to its referent** | **Examples** |
|---|---|---|
| **Icon** | **resemblance** — looks like what it means | film itself, drawings, photographs, screen images |
| **Index** | **causal** connection | smoke↔fire, a speedometer, windblown trees, a shadow (a footprint is *both* icon and index) |
| **Symbol** | **arbitrary convention** — must be taught | language, religious/organisational symbols |

Ultimately the **audience decides** the meaning — so you must teach them what your signs mean before you rely on them.

## Significant objects

Semiotics lets a film refer to what is **not on screen**, visually and without words. Hitchcock's three object types are the canonical menu:

- **McGuffin** — an object everyone *wants* that sets the story in motion; its actual nature is irrelevant ("a McGuffin is nothing at all"). Dumbo's "Magic Feather" is a placebo McGuffin — it means nothing except that it lets him believe.
- **Symbolic object** — becomes dangerous once you know what it signifies. The ring in *Shadow of a Doubt* travels from gift → object of suspicion → object of horror → proof of the uncle's guilt, its meaning shifting with information.
- **Threatening / oppressive object** — menaces by sheer silent presence (the *Psycho* house; height as threat in *Vertigo*, *North by Northwest*, *Saboteur*).

Objects (and even **names** — "Darth Vader" ≈ dark father/invader) can silently state love, death, time, character, and class; doors, windows, and mirrors are especially eloquent about states of mind.

## How images ask questions

The director presents images that pose questions driven by character desire, wish, and fear — then **delays the answers**. The audience is always asking:

- What is it? What is going on?
- Why do I care? Why *should* I care?
- What do the characters want?
- What will happen next? What may change to set something in motion?

## Speaking indirectly — the four master tropes

Real persuasive power comes *after* clarity: once you can speak directly, you learn to speak **indirectly**. All tropes have a visual form, and they reduce to four:

- **Metaphor** — maps a *known* thing onto an *unknown* one for a flash of insight (iconic, based on resemblance). Two consecutive shots imply comparison — a plane, then a bird, says *the plane is like a bird*. Metaphor lets concrete pictures express **abstract** ideas (theme, inner state, power relations).
- **Metonymy** — one signified stands for another *closely associated* with it (indexical; "the evocation of the whole by a connection"; effect-for-cause). An object stands for its owner; a pan along a body links each part to the next (works even if "Frankensteined" from different bodies). Enables **metonymical misdirection** (the "Forty Sail" gag; *Holy Grail*'s coconut horses).
- **Synecdoche** — the **part for the whole** (often treated as a subclass of metonymy). A **close-up is a simple synecdoche**; the very frame is synecdoche — it offers a "slice of life" and implies the world continues outside it. All representation involves synecdoche because it can only *select*.
- **Irony** — the most radical trope: the signifier seems to mean one thing while another signifier tells us it means the opposite. Irony therefore needs **two signifiers** — one that states, one that qualifies it as ironic (a knowing smile, or the context). **Ironic juxtaposition** lets one shot comment on the next — powerful for scene transitions.

## Speaking indirectly in time

Meaning is **retroactive**: once shown, an image is still affected by what comes after, and the signified stays fluid until *anchored* at the end. Mysteries exploit this — a clue attaches to one suspect, new context shifts it to another, and only the ending anchors every signified into place (the *Shadow of a Doubt* ring, once its full journey is known).

## Everything speaks, if you know the code

**Codes** are learned so invisibly they feel neutral; they set the **genre** and the context for reading action (a gun behaving like it belongs in a horror film breaks the Western's code). *The Matrix* works because it couches a new world in codes we already own (superhero-comic, phone-booth transformation); *The Golden Compass* left one director cold because it renamed familiar things ("souls" → "demons") without teaching the audience its codes — *"a foreign film without subtitles."* Codes can also **critique** a genre (*Unforgiven* deglamorising the gunfighter). **Codes specific to film** are a small vocabulary worth naming: cross-dissolve = time passing; split screen = simultaneity; slow motion = extreme action or danger. (The **semiotic square** — positive value, its contrary, the negation of the negation, the negative value — is Greimas's/McKee's tool for mapping a theme's variations across scenes and characters.)

## Studio application

- **Ch. 9 is the studio's argument that a `Shot` must *carry* a story point — it grounds the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py)'s read of whether an image *communicates*, not merely whether it's pretty.** Denotation/connotation and the icon/index/symbol split give the reconciler an explicit vocabulary for "does this frame *mean* what the beat needs?" — the same Observer→Storyteller judgement that motivates a persona reconciler over this Directing grounding rather than a purely mechanical merge of owned fields ([storyline 0016](../../../context/storyline/0016-abridging-the-screenwriters-taxonomy.md)).
- **"Significant object" and "point at the clue" are direct prompt/render legibility constraints.** Making a not-present thing readable through an on-screen sign (a locket, a checklist, a bundle of sticks) is a demand on [`prompt.py`](../../../sequitur/prompt.py) and [`image.py`](../../../sequitur/image.py): the meaning-bearing object must be *composed to read* (dominance, contrast, the eye-direction craft of [Ch. 7](ch07-how-to-direct-the-eyes.md)), or the sign is missed. A shot whose story-object doesn't register is a legibility failure to catch on the [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py), not a tuning problem.
- **The four tropes are *edit-and-juxtaposition* tools, so this chapter reaches past the single [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py) into the sequence.** Metaphor ("plane then bird"), metonymy, and ironic juxtaposition are meanings made *between* shots — they belong to how the editorial layer orders and adjoins frames, and to the retroactive-meaning logic the edit executes. Keep this as the semantic layer above the DP's per-shot grammar in [`crew/camera.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py).
- **"Teach your codes" is a consistency constraint across a whole [`studio`](../../../sequitur/studio.py) production.** Because the audience decides meaning and codes must be established before they're relied on, a sign planted early must stay legible and consistent across shots — an argument for production-scope continuity of significant objects, not per-shot re-invention.

Reconcile when meaning is encoded: the DP composes a *legible* frame, the Director loads it with a *readable sign*.

This closes the "directing the eye" cluster — attention, depth, and meaning are now three layers of one craft: stage the eye, locate it in space, and make what it lands on *speak*.
