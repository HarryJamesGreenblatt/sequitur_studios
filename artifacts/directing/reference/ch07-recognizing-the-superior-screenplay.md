# Chapter 7 — Recognizing the Superior Screenplay

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 7.
> **Scope:** The director's craft of **judging** a screenplay — trusting first impressions, assessing technical and cinematic craftsmanship, interrogating embedded values, spotting archetypes, and testing viability against real resources — to decide whether to commit. This grounds the Director's *script-judgment* function.

## Core idea

The moment a screenplay lands, the director begins assessing its **quality, movie potential, and feasibility**, and must decide to proceed or keep looking. First impressions and intuitions are precious because they mirror an ordinary audience's — take careful notes and interrogate them. A **superior screenplay** tells its story mainly through revealing behavior and action (not dialogue-as-narration), carries **subtext** and organic **metaphor**, conveys a **central idea** on the very first read, honestly reflects the world's values, and — decisively — can be made with the resources you actually have.

## Trust the first impression

The first read-through is your **only** chance at an audience-like first impression, so guard it. Read from beginning to end **without stopping**, as you'd watch a film in a theatre — for **story and pleasure**, not analysis. Then digest and record your intuitions: How did it hit you? What taste did it leave, what feelings did it arouse, what operative words spring to mind? **Expect contradictions** — you can intellectually reject a work's characters or milieu yet weep for them at the end. Those contradictions are signal, not noise.

A good screenplay conveys a **central idea** — a thematic core that gives the story its ultimate meaning — audibly on the first reading. Note it: it is your **guiding beacon** through every later change. If it's great, protect it from dilution; if it's weak, plan to strengthen it in later drafts.

## Script language and technique

A script must at minimum get the **technical** aspects right. Professional readers won't finish a script whose first pages show poor formatting, wrong script language, or typos — carelessness there predicts careless storytelling. The professionally written screenplay is deliberately **minimal**: it seeds a longer process whose real product is told in images and sounds.

**Stage directions** — spare yet vivid; only the essential locations, actions, images, and sounds that advance the story. **Overwriting** ("directing on the page" — excess environment, blocking, and gesture) is tedious and *dangerous*: it conditions readers, actors, and crew to expect hard-edged results and locks the director into a variable-free vision. Leave the light source to the cinematographer, wall color and set dressing to the designer, and *how* to look nervous to the director and actor. **Never** write what a character thinks or feels — film can only tell its story through **recordable actions, images, and sounds**; a page of characters silently "thinking" shoots as two people in a room doing nothing.

**Dialogue and personal directions** — must never restate what the camera shows or repeat what the action makes obvious. Poor dialogue is overwritten and repetitive. Don't render accents phonetically (awkward, even insulting); instead specify the accent and write the line normally, and imply it through **vernacular grammar** ("Thank you for the advices"). "Directing on the page" also invades dialogue via verbal tics, dictated pauses, and dictated inflection — every such control **closes an interpretive door** for the actor.

## First reading → margin notes

After the uninterrupted first read, note your intuitions against a checklist:

- Did you like it? Did you connect — and how?
- What did it make you feel? Which characters did you care about or find fascinating?
- What was the plot about? Which moments hit hardest?
- What felt like a chore? What didn't ring true?
- **Beneath the surface events, what is the piece really about?**

Then read once or twice more hunting **hard evidence**: *where* in the script did each impression arise, and *why*? Make **margin notes** tracking your moment-by-moment reactions. Over the process these notes evolve into a map of what you want your **audience** to feel, think, decide, and understand at each moment.

## Assessing cinematic qualities

**How is the story told?** Locate where meaning arises and ask *how* it is conveyed — through action and behavior, or through dialogue? Cinema is **stronger** when it reveals character through behavior and action, **weaker** when it uses dialogue to narrate thoughts, feelings, and situations. Dialogue should never substitute for action; used only when truly necessary, it should **reveal, not explain** — the expression of human will (characters acting upon each other), not exposition.

**Integrity of characters and motives.** Trace major events and characters **backward** to check the groundwork is laid. Drama built on coincidence, on a character wheeled out purely for plot, or on a convenient plot-changing detail reads as **shoddy and contrived**.

**Subtext.** In life people rarely address the true source of their tensions — they don't know it, or they hide it. Drama exploits this as **displacement**. **Subtext** is the unspoken (often subconscious) meaning and motive behind a character's words or actions, tied to the character's through-line and revealed only through behavior and context. In *Sideways*, Miles ostensibly praises the pinot noir grape but is really describing his own thin-skinned, uncomprehended self and asking Maya to understand his potential; in *The Social Network*, Sean's flat question about whether Eduardo came to California carries a whole subtext of usurpation that a simple head-shake completes. When director and actors **know** the subtext, they can build behaviors that manifest the tension between inner and outer worlds.

**Metaphoric detail.** Settings, objects, and character detail can serve as symbolic or metaphoric keys to deeper issues — but they must be **organic** to the characters' world or the audience gags on the manipulative symbol and the over-earnest metaphor. The bleached desert of *Paris, Texas* emblematizes a man's emotional aridity; the savage seashore and forbidden piano of *The Piano* announce, from the earliest scenes, a story about the overwhelming power of nature and elemental passion.

## Checking the embedded values

All storytelling starts from assumptions about what will seem "normal" to an audience. Stereotypes and clichés arise from **embedded values** — assumptions so familiar to the makers that they pass **below awareness**. Interrogating them (a grid adapted from *Creative Filmmaking from the Inside Out*, Dannenbaum, Hodge & Mayer) is not about political correctness but about **not presenting as "normal" what shouldn't be** — and examining where your own cultural assumptions came from. Fresher, more authentic characters and situations are the reward.

| Domain | Interrogate |
|---|---|
| **Characters** | Class; wealth (and how it's regarded); reliability of appearances; background/diversity of race; belongings and work (do they fit the character's means?); talismans (significant objects); valuation (for what are they valued, and does the film question it?); speech (what vocabulary betrays); roles (complex enough to break stereotype?); sexuality and gender roles (range, tenderness vs. disembodied lust); volition (who can change their situation?); competence (who is capable, and why?). |
| **Environment** | Place (origin values); settings (credible? additive?); time (period values); home (do characters seem at home, and what surrounds them?); work (how the workplace is portrayed). |
| **Family dynamics** | Structure (normal/abnormal, and who's critical of it?); relationships across members and generations; roles (fixed or developing, healthy or not); power (could another structure exist? money↔power). |
| **Authority** | Gender (which has most authority, why?); initiation (who starts events, who resolves them?); respect (how power and institutions are depicted); conflict (how negotiated, who wins and why?); violence (justified, or a crutch for excitement — and what does gratuitous violence say about your values?). |
| **In total** | Criticism (how critical is the film of what characters do?); approval/disapproval (does it challenge the audience's assumptions or just feed them?); world view (what the microcosm implies about the macrocosm); moral stance (its belief system re privilege, willpower, tradition, power, luck, coincidence — is this what you want?). |

To make fiction is to **propose a reality** — true of fantasy and realism alike. Beyond guarding against mistakes, use this grid to consciously **further your deepest intentions**.

## Spotting archetypes

Every narrative echoes older ones whether the writer intends it or not. Myths, legends, and folktales are a free "savings bank" of resonant character types, proven themes, and satisfying structures — potent, adaptable, and **owned by no one**.

| Form | Character |
|---|---|
| **Legends** | Purposefully *inauthentic history* — the exceptional deeds of real people, embellished by generations. Carry enduring themes (love, loyalty, betrayal, pride, cowardice, courage). The Arthurian cycle spans romantic history, tragic adultery, and outright slapstick. |
| **Fables / folktales** | Teaching stories told to entertain **and** impart moral lessons and survival skills. (Ashliman's *Folklore and Mythology Electronic Texts* catalogs hundreds worldwide.) |
| **Myths** | Often invoke the supernatural; deal lyrically with insoluble aspects of human nature and offer **no neat outcome**. Their fallible gods dramatize heroism, transgression, hubris, illusion. *Moonlight* echoes the centaur Chiron — the wild figure civilized by a wise protector. |

A film need not be a **direct adaptation** to resonate: invoking a single character or one aspect of a tale (as *The Fisher King* borrows the Grail quest and courtly love) enriches a contemporary story with centuries of subtext.

## Viability and working within limitations

If each read draws you **deeper** — imagining scenes, hearing voices, seeing movement — pause and ask the sobering question: **is this do-able with my resources?** Answer realistically, not optimistically.

**Story givens** (the practical parameters directly specified in the script):

- **Epoch** (contemporary or period) and **total time frame**.
- **Length** (remember 1 page ≈ 1 minute).
- **Tone** (naturalistic, hyperbolic, fantastic…).
- **Locations** — where and how many.
- **Character details** — total count; the main character; other majors; minors (speaking or not); extras.
- **Wardrobe and essential props**.
- **Number of scenes** and time of day for each.

You can tweak some givens (combine or cut characters/scenes, swap a location), but most define the scale of the story and the resources it demands.

**Project resources** (balance strong, connecting material against what you can realistically accomplish):

- **Financial** — cash on hand, cash you can realistically raise, plus in-kind support (donated equipment, services, supplies).
- **Production time** — school/professional deadlines; cast and crew availability; experience level (inexperience slows everything).
- **Equipment and supplies** — what's available and what you're qualified to use.
- **Locations, sets, props, wardrobe** — extent of requirements; geographic limits; can you create or access them?
- **Cast and crew** — size, experience, and whether you can actually cast it.

*(Cautionary case: a powerful short script hinged on a real two-month-old baby that no parent would loan — the film was never made. Be brutally realistic about requirements before committing.)*

**Viability and safety.** Take safety seriously in the assessment. Car chases, picture vehicles, firearms, stunts, fire, water near electricity, boats, extreme weather, or risky locations all demand specialized equipment, professional oversight, and money — and may run afoul of school, state, or union rules. Look for anything that pushes past common sense.

**Working within limitations.** *Every* film — student exercise to Hollywood tentpole — works within limits; a good idea that exceeds your resources yields **no film at all**. Understand and build with what you **have**, not what you wish you had — and treat limitation as **creative opportunity**. Ana Lily Amirpour's *A Girl Walks Home Alone at Night* (a black-and-white, Farsi-language Iranian vampire-western, first feature) was written around resources already in reach — known locations and actors, a car, a cat — then crowdfunded from her own network with clever, near-zero-cost perks (naming streets in the fictional "Bad City"). The stylish result cost under \$60,000 and earned her a budget a hundred times larger next time.
