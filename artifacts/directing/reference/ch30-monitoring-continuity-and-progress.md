# Chapter 30 — Monitoring Continuity and Progress

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 30.
> **Scope:** the **script (continuity) supervisor** — the person who guards against omissions and mismatches, tracks coverage and timing, logs continuity and dialogue, and files the report that follows the footage to the editor — plus the director's parallel job of monitoring dramatic quality, authorial intent, and resources. This is the **SEAM between shoot and edit.**

## Core idea

A director's recurring nightmare is to find, after cast and crew have gone, that a **vital angle was overlooked**. Mistakes multiply in scenes of several simultaneous actions (crowds, fights) whose relationships must match shot to shot, and in moving dialogue scenes that cross the axis or skip reaction shots. Fatigue and last-minute changes raise the odds everywhere, and hand-to-mouth low-budget work — too few people, too many tasks, a checklist so rewritten it becomes a hazard — is most exposed. The guard against all of it is the **script supervisor** (continuity supervisor): the person who tracks **every shot covered** and **every continuity issue**, and who is in some ways the closest person to the director during shooting. If you have none, use the **editor** — they have more reason than anyone to get it right. (Rabiger credits Pat P. Miller's *Script Supervising and Film Continuity* for the procedures below.)

Read across the studio's own architecture, this chapter grounds the **production→postproduction handoff**: continuity notes and coverage logs are the record of *what is actually in the footage*, and that record is exactly what the Editor role reads to assemble the cut.

## The script supervisor prepares

Continuity work begins the moment a finished script exists. The supervisor **reads, analyzes, breaks down, and rereads** until every need is memorized, then produces the working records:

- **Script notes.** A first breakdown lists **locations** and **characters** — names, characteristics, physical attributes, overt actions, entries and exits. A scene breakdown lays scenes out in order, each with location, time of day, characters, chronological data, script pages, and length. This culminates in **one or more sheets per camera setup** — records that guide the shoot and are then used **intensively by the editor**.
- **Chronology.** A thorough story chronology is built. If the story is told out of order (flashbacks, flash-forwards), this has profound consequences for the **age or condition** of characters and thus their makeup and costuming. Continuity must always know where the story is **spatially and temporally**, what befell characters before we see them, and where they go after.
- **Coordinating and communicating.** As coordinator of countless details, the supervisor gathers specifics from **wardrobe, props, and makeup** and makes sure the PM has the right details per scene. The production office then issues the daily **call sheet** — scenes, actors, costuming, properties, equipment, transport, special provisions, and emergency contacts for the next day.

## Monitoring coverage and timing

The supervisor must be intimately familiar with the **coverage plan** as an editing and shooting strategy — knowing how each dramatic beat is covered, anticipating edit points where **shot-to-shot continuity matters most**, and updating the shooting script as changes occur. The marking system:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Solid line** through action/dialogue | That material **was covered** — the line also records *how* it was covered and *how many takes* it took. |
| **Squiggly interruption** in the line | Action or dialogue that **occurred during the shot but wasn't visible** to the camera from that angle (therefore not covered). |
| **Notes alongside** | Shot timings, take counts, and any **change to planned coverage** (e.g. merging two shots into one with a camera move). |

By day's end the supervisor holds a record of **exactly what was shot** — down to variant dialogue lines — so anyone can see at a glance how a scene was covered and what remains.

**Timing.** A **stopwatch** tracks scene lengths, because scenes persistently running long yield an overlong film — whole scenes then dropped in the cutting room, with an irate producer asking why they were shot at all. Screen time is predicted by **page count** (one page ≈ one minute of screen time), specified to the nearest **eighth of a page**. Master scenes give the first overall timing; **every take of every shot** is logged. Tracking **pages shot and screen time completed** per day tells the unit whether it is lagging schedule or exceeding length. The eighths system matters on complex feature schedules (measuring page count to fractions to schedule hours and days); for short films that granularity is unnecessary.

## Types of continuity

**Continuity** is the match of details from scene to scene and cut to cut. When a chronologically **later** scene is shot **before** an earlier one, continuity must be **back-matched** to preserve compatibility. Two modes:

- **Direct continuity** — one shot or scene immediately follows another. A character **cannot change jackets** stepping between rooms.
- **Indirect continuity** — between scenes separated by time or by other scenes. A man goes carousing and we see him hours later: same clothes, now rumpled and stained, face shadowed with stubble. In **parallel storytelling** (intercutting an A story and a B story), indirect continuity must hold across **all** the A segments and **all** the B segments.

The supervisor watches for **everything an alert audience will ever notice** — sometimes requiring research (someone will always point out that a cigarette brand didn't exist in the period, or a bird never makes its mating call in the depicted month).

## Wardrobe, property, and physical continuity

The supervisor keeps a hawk eye on **costume, hair, makeup, and property** continuity. Three classes of property:

| Class | What it is | Continuity concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Hand props** | Handled by characters (comb, diary). | State and handedness must match across coverage. |
| **Stage props** | Part of an environment (lava lamp, princess bed); may belong to a character. | Placement and dressing stay consistent. |
| **Breakaway props** | Broken or used up (pottery figure, foam cup). | **Replacements must be on hand** for subsequent takes. |

**Physical continuity** tracks whether a beer glass was full or half-finished, how a jacket was buttoned, a scarf tied, a cake sliced. If a character lifts wine with her **right** hand in the master but her **left** in the closer shot, or **rises during** a line in the medium but **after** it in the long shot, there is "wailing and gnashing of teeth in the cutting room." Continuity's job is to **alert everyone to lapses** so they're corrected or their editing options known — meaning being very prepared and very observant *all* the time. **Digital snaps** of characters and sets before and after each take keep tabs on wardrobe, hair, and makeup; shooting on video makes some of this less necessary (footage settles the actor certain his jacket was unbuttoned).

## Monitoring dialogue and where the supervisor works

From take to take the supervisor **logs every word the actors use** and records **variations** that may create editing problems — plot information is often embedded in dialogue, so it would be disastrous to keep a take in which the detective names the wrong suspect. They may also note relative **pacing, subtext, and feeling** across takes.

Positioning varies: many now watch on a **dedicated field monitor**, replaying takes for questions and grabbing screen stills for a visual record; on extensive handheld shoots (camera untethered) they **shadow the operator closely**; some old-school supervisors prefer to **sit beside the camera** for a wider, more direct perspective. Between takes you'll find the supervisor **at the director's elbow**, confirming what's shot, what's next, and the prognosis for finishing the day's setups on time.

## The script supervisor's report — the liaison to post

At each day's end the supervisor prepares a **report that follows the footage to the editor**. These reports are critical for postproduction because they record **what is actually in the footage** — the good, the bad, and the altered — helping the editor **review and organize** the raw material. In this the supervisor is the **all-important liaison between the production and postproduction teams**. The scope of the job is "nothing short of super-human"; dedicated software (e.g. **ScriptE**) helps, but it remains a job of pencils, paper, ruler, stopwatch, binder, total attention to detail, and formidable perception.

## Monitoring your own progress as director

Dramatic quality tops the audience's concerns — consistent, convincing performances, credible action, mood-enhancing lighting, responsive camera, clean dialogue — yet you can only concentrate on **actors and camera movement**. Three levels of oversight:

- **Reviewing takes on the set.** Helpful, but a **nervous habit** wastes hours on tiny imperfections (a light glare, an errant lock of hair) that go unnoticed in the edited scene, and glues you to the monitor, disconnecting you from the actors. And **don't let actors watch their work** — they turn impulsively self-critical (the lead who caught himself with "lazy mouth" and thereafter over-articulated).
- **Screening dailies.** Reserve time at day's end to screen **select takes**, ideally with sync sound, so **reshooting can happen before the set is struck** or lighting becomes irreproducible. Even a dog-tired unit finds enthusiasm for its own work. Exclude actors politely but firmly — "a technical check for the unit only."
- **Screening scene assemblies.** Portable editing lets the **editor accompany the unit and ingest takes as they arrive**, assembling a day's work in an hour or two so the unit sees rough outline **before the set is struck**. Many **mismatches show up only in edited form** — inconsistencies in acting, lighting, framing, sound, or continuity — which can then be improved in subsequent shooting. The director sees whether performances are consistent and pitched right and whether stylistic intentions work. A rough assembly of the **whole movie** should exist within days of wrap; if the editor can't be on location, assemblies can be posted online.

## Fulfilling your authorship intentions

Directing film actors means getting each character's precise interior and exterior aims to **show**. It is taxing and easily derailed, so **keep nothing in your head that can go onto paper**. **Lists save your life** when the "fatal fog" of fatigue descends: make crib notes the night before, check them at the scene's start and again at its end — *Did you cover every item? Are there fresh consequences for scenes that follow?* Fatigue and its oblivion are the director's biggest hazard: the industrial rhythm of production makes attention lapse. The two haunting questions — *Am I fulfilling my authorial intentions?* and *Do I have a film?* — resolve into specific areas of examination:

| Area | What to interrogate |
|---|---|
| **Dramatic clarity** | The sign of effective work is that, by the camera, **you spontaneously feel what the audience will feel**. When actors truly feel, you feel it; if you're *searching* for the expected, something's missing — lead the actors psychically to a new place where they reconnect (clues come from the rehearsal history). |
| **Subtext** | Every take is **unpredictable of outcome**, demanding high concentration. Identical action and dialogue can play as "two losers sizing each other up" or "two depressed people wondering if it's worth talking" — different, **actual and ephemeral** subtexts you catch only if alert to the moment. |
| **Scene dialectics** | Repetition lulls scenes into a **mesmerized, singsong** state. Keep the **dialectics** evident — the opposing polarities of will and opinion that set person against person, idea against idea, a person against himself. These irresolvable tensions "stand out like spars in a majestic bridge." |

Interrogating your psyche is the only way into the "sealed room" where your intuitive self already registered the scene's meaning. At each juncture, **assess whether you won or lost** — hard, lonely work, because what's before the camera often underwhelms. Note too that **dailies reveal more than you saw live**: a bravura performance can read as **hamming** on screen. If you suspect it while shooting, call more takes for **contained, sincere** emotion — the less convinced you feel, the more you **cover yourself with alternatives** for editing later.

## Resources and cost reports

The director handles the human and ineffable, but every decision becomes **bills, costs, rates, and schedules**. Whatever order you shoot in, the **line producer or PM should compute daily** where the production stands against budget, and you should be told **every day** whether you're near or over. Overspending forces a choice: **raise more money, economize back onto track, or drop the least vital scenes** (maybe shoot the last scene without the crane). Beware the common trap: **liberal coverage early, perilously thin late** — late coverage may then be editable only one way, if at all. Budget and monitor resources as you spend them, or the shoot becomes "an expedition that leaves home eating steak and then boils its shoes to stay alive." When shooting ends the toll is severe — many directors go low or physically ill for a week or two ("postpartum depression").

## At the end of production

You had an **icebreaker** before shooting; now hold a **get-together at wrap** to thank and congratulate everyone (a coordinated potluck if money's low). Notice how much freer everyone is after the shared journey of battles and fellowships — and ask **which of these people you'd like to work with again**, and what ideas are taking shape for the next story.

## Studio application

- **This chapter *is* the shoot→edit seam in code.** The script supervisor's end-of-day report — "a record of **what is actually in the footage** (the good, the bad, and the altered)" — is precisely the input the Editor consumes to build the EDL: coverage logs become `Clip`s, continuity notes become the constraints on how they join. The **solid line = covered / squiggly = not visible** marking is the production-side ledger of the coverage that [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py) later assumes exists; where the ledger is short, `Sequence.validate()` in [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py) is where the gap surfaces as a lint error rather than a cutting-room surprise.
- **Continuity is a first-class edit motivator.** "Matched action, screen direction, and position keep the flow invisible" is `EditReason.CONTINUITY` verbatim in [editorial.py](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py); "the best place to cut is on a strong physical movement" is `EditCategory.ACTION` (the match cut). **Breakaway-prop replacements** and hand/stage-prop matching are the physical-world guarantee that a match cut *can* be made — the same role the `head_handle`/`tail_handle` overlaps on a `Clip` play for a dissolve. This is *Grammar of the Edit*'s "**assessing footage**" ([ch04-assessing-footage](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch04-assessing-footage.md)) seen from the **set side**, and its "reason for every edit" ([ch08-editors-mindset](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch08-editors-mindset.md), [ch05-when-to-cut](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md)). *(Overlap flag: continuity/coverage assessment here ↔ Grammar of the Edit Ch. 1/4/5/8 — one is the record produced on set, the other the assessment performed in post.)*
- **Timing = runtime.** "One page ≈ one minute" and the stopwatch logging of every take are the human ancestor of `Clip.duration` and `Sequence.runtime` in [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py): the supervisor's screen-time tally is the schedule/length check the studio makes structural. The director's crib-note checklist — *did you cover every item?* — is the completeness check `Director.reconcile()` performs when it demands every owned `Shot` field be filled ([director.py](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py)).
- **Cross-department through-lines.** Upstream, the coverage plan the supervisor tracks *is* the shooting script of [ch24](ch24-developing-the-shooting-script.md), executed under [ch29](ch29-directing-on-the-set.md); the call sheet is the producing team's board. Downstream, the report hands off to the Editor of [ch31](ch31-postproduction-overview.md) — the "liaison between production and postproduction" is, in the codebase, the boundary between `Phase.SHOOT` and `Phase.ASSEMBLE` ([role.py](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py)). Dialogue variation logging also feeds sound post ([ch10-postproduction-workflow](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch10-postproduction-workflow.md)).

*The footage is logged, timed, and matched; next, [Ch. 31](ch31-postproduction-overview.md) hands it to the editor and the alchemy begins.*
