# Chapter 33 — The Rough Cut

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 33.
> **Scope:** the heart of editing — cutting the rough cut so each scene finds its right place and succeeds dramatically. Editing as an observing consciousness; repairing performance; modulating subtext through timing; counterpoint, split edits, sound bridges, and flow. The director's-eye complement to *Grammar of the Edit* Chs. 5–8.

## Core idea

A **rough cut** is an evolving version of the film that deploys the full range of storytelling technique to implement the goals set after scrutinizing the assembly. As Thelma Schoonmaker frames the editor's contribution: you shape the movie and the performances, give the work rhythm and pace, and sometimes adjust the structure so the film starts to *flow* on the screen. Rough cuts are built from **picture and audio essentials only** — dialogue cut with picture, voice-over where it drives, and a temporary **scratch track** to stand in for music — while the editor works to make each scene occupy its right place and land dramatically. **Resist finessing**: no layering soundtracks, no placing effects, no sweating ultrafine points, since most will be undone. Expect several rough-cut versions, each tightened by a critical screening so the next is an *improvement*, not merely a different arrangement of the same faults.

## Editing mimics an observing consciousness

The rough-cut stage is where the story's integrity, structure, subtexts, tone, dramatic arcs, and themes take their true cinematic form. The governing model is **human consciousness at work**: in a dialogue scene we cut between speakers based on **whatever would make an observer shift their attention**. Eye contact and eye-line shifts are the outward signs of those attention shifts, so editing to the characters' eye-lines works from human behavior rather than disembodied theory. Walter Murch's insight that the **blink** is an unconscious punctuation of our thoughts is a key to dialogue editing. Silent film had to **juxtapose** shots to imply continuity, contrast, and relatedness — the audience guessed the associations — and much of our pleasure still comes from hypothesizing subtext. **Counterpointing** sound against action (rather than using sound merely to accompany or illustrate) came later, developed largely by documentary editors compressing life; Altman's densely layered dialogue tracks (*M\*A\*S\*H*, *Nashville*, *Gosford Park*) place great faith in the audience's ability to interpret.

### How we use eye contact

Eye contact in life is widely misunderstood. Lovers who gaze soulfully into each other's eyes while speaking read as **phony** — they're playing an *idea* of conversation. Real people make eye contact only fleetingly, reserving its intensity for pivotal moments — to:

- **Check** the effect we just had.
- **Get information** from the other's expression so we know how to act on them next.
- **Apply pressure** — a plea or a challenge.
- **Interpret** body language or expression when we are being acted upon.

Each speaker alternates between acting and being acted upon; much of the time their gaze rests on neutral objects while they glean what the other wants — which is why those very human **cutaways** can carry symbolic meaning on film. Hearing behaves differently — it can stay fully focused for a whole conversation, yet it too wanders. **Eyes and ears move their attention independently**, feeding separate streams into an overworked brain (watch a hunting cat's eyes and ears do overtly what we do subtly).

### Eyelines, the Concerned Observer, and character psychology

Editing draws on two interpretive positions (Ch. 16): the **Concerned Observer** and the **Storyteller**. Watch a real conversation and keep an interior running commentary; you'll notice that you **follow eye-line shifts** (wanting to see what they see), that **each shift makes you hypothesize a new subtext**, that there is a **rhythm** controlled by the conversation's own tensions, and that you make instinctive judgments about who and what to look at. **Watching becomes thinking, and thinking develops meanings** for which the Observer seeks confirmation. To reproduce this on film you cover each speaker from the other's viewpoint, add a third angle to embrace them both, and complement with over-the-shoulder angles and close-ups — the basic coverage model. Because **wherever a person looks implies what they think and feel**, editing becomes a powerful tool to imply inner life, moving viewpoint from objective to subjective and structuring **whose POV and whose state of mind** the audience shares. The **Concerned Observer becomes a Storyteller** whenever dedicated watching turns into an engaged, critical, partisan narrative that shares ideas about who the characters are and what they want.

## Repairing performance issues

Seen in context, small inadequacies surface that weren't evident on set — mismatched moods and tempos, variable intensity and believability, lines that fail to deliver. Inexperienced actors, repeating lines across takes and angles, tend to drop into a **shared set rhythm** that homogenizes their inner lives into a monotonous average and can dominate — and deaden — an early cut. **Small, strategic edits can repair wayward performances** and recover the spontaneity of people thinking on their feet (though flat-out bad performance throughout cannot be fixed in post). The editor's repertoire:

- **Trim or cut** dialogue that strains believability, where no critical information is lost.
- **Redistribute lines** to build a better emotional arc.
- **Polarize** the scene toward the strongest, truest character; play the others as reaction shots or voices off.
- **Draw a performance from multiple takes** — grab the strongest moments across takes to construct an arc; sometimes use only the **audio** of a take.
- **Add reaction shots, glances, cutaways** to reduce the offending actor's on-screen time — a strong listener or a revealing detail can supply the correct tension or subtext.
- **Recalibrate rhythm** with reaction shots and cutaways to shorten or extend the pauses between lines.

The archetypal fix: an Executive's bad-news monologue where only take 1's **audio** was good and take 3's **picture** usable. Syncing the first lines, then cutting to the managers' nervous reactions *during* the off-screen speech (rather than sequentially, as scripted), then back for one last stern look — the scene not only worked but became **leaner and more powerful**. Even with merely *indifferent* (not bad) performances, editing to **develop latent subtexts** can drive up intensity and stakes until the scene becomes a unity nobody foresaw.

## Editing to develop or alter subtexts

For any adequately staged, performed, and shot scene, much dramatic control now rests in the cutting room — **who or what is shown, and the timing and pace.** Performance timing originates with the actors and is fixed in an unedited single take, but **given coverage, dialogue timing can be adjusted significantly** — especially **reaction time**, the inner process preceding outward action, which shapes a scene's subtext. "Do you think it's cold outside?" can mean *should I dress warmer?*, *let's not go*, or *stay the night* — and what makes the audience choose is **how the listener reacts**: an easy "No" differs from one after a silence or a long internal debate, and prior events condition the reading. **The order of material and the apparent thought each character devotes imply very different subtexts**, generating the open questions the Storyteller always wants.

| Cut of "Let's just go home" | Effect on subtext |
|---|---|
| **(a) Master two-shot, unedited** | Fixed pace; the interaction cannot be re-timed. |
| **(b) Cut to his CU, pause trimmed** | Quick reply → a whiff of impatience or dismissiveness; cutting before her line finishes reads as an *interruption* (greater irritation). |
| **(c) Combine pauses from master + CU** | Doubled reaction time → weight and deliberation; doubling reaction shots stretches it into a long moment heavy with unspoken concern. |

Careful rebalancing of reaction times adds massively to the credibility of a character's inner life — **new rhythms aid and abet performance and give the viewer grounds to infer greater thought and feeling.**

## Rhythm: an analogy in music

Everything in editing happens in minutes, seconds, and frames, yet **you can no more make dramatic decisions from a stopwatch than compose music with a metronome.** An edited conversation runs **two interlocked rhythms** — the ebb and flow of the voices, and the visual tempo of cutting, composition, and movement — proceeding independently yet related, like music to two dancers.

- **Harmony** — hearing a speaker while seeing their face; sound allied to vision like melody with its harmony.
- **Counterpoint** — sound from one shot against image from another. Cut from a woman speaking of her vanished husband to a pan across tawdry seaside hotels while her words play on; our mind's eye imagines where he is. (Merely cutting to the hotels on "the last I heard, he was in Florida" would only *illustrate* — a location change.)
- **Dissonance** — a deliberate discrepancy that spurs the viewer to resolve it: a salesman preaching dynamic persuasion (heard) over his own dreary monotone boring a customer into a trance (seen) → "here is someone who does not know himself."

## Transitions, split edits, and sound bridges

Life's transitions are imperceptible when routine and jarring when they surprise; stories either **hide the seams** or **emphasize** them. An **action match** (morning fruit juice → a beer raised in a dive) links by contrast; a **dissolve** creakily announces "time passed"; a **straight cut** leaves the audience to fill the blank; a **shock cut** (a bored teen singing along to the radio, then flash-cut truck, screeching tires, wrenched wheel) reproduces being taken nastily by surprise. Each transition, like the phrase "and then …," implies an attitude emanating from the characters or the Storyteller.

**The split edit** (also **lap cut** / **L-cut**) cuts audio and picture at **different times** — incoming audio preceding or trailing its image — breaking the staid, predictable blocks that simultaneous straight cuts produce. In a father/son argument, bringing the **son's** image in early (while the father still protests) privileges the son's POV; bringing the **father's** image in *twice* while the son speaks favors the father's — **the same footage, radically different perspective**, provided the coverage exists.

**Why split edits work:** in a real conversation you seldom turn your head at the exact moment the next speaker begins — only an omniscient being could. **Level cuts** between speakers give a prepackaged, premeditated look; that mechanical omniscience destroys the illusion of spontaneous development. Staggered cuts replicate an **agile, questing consciousness** as eyes chase hearing, or hearing catches up late with something just seen. **Effective editing is always grounded in the unfolding consciousness of the characters.**

**Lap-cut scene transitions** cure the most banal scene change — a character exits to an empty set, cut to an empty frame, then reappears (a 19th-century proscenium hiccup). A tidy lap cut both smooths the transition and can create a **revealing juxtaposition** (a father's line "Once you go in — you never come out" laid under the shot of a hospital). *The Silence of the Lambs* runs Crawford's uncaptioned "never forget what he is" straight into Chilton's off-screen "Oh, he's a monster" over the exterior of the asylum — an **audio-first lap cut** that introduces the squeeze of male authority.

**Scene elision with sound** uses sound effects the same way:

- **Anticipatory sound** — a rising playground din heard in the teacher's bedroom before we cut to her at the school door; curiosity resolves the riddle, so the switch never feels arbitrary.
- **Holdover sound** — dreary class chanting that persists as she picks at leftovers at home, subsiding slowly.

Both are **sound bridges** — sound connecting two very different locations, softening the transition and implying psychology and inner consciousness. Used inventively, creative sound transport the viewer forward without cumbersome opticals, scattering clues about characters' subjective lives that film cannot otherwise easily convey.

## The problem of achieving flow

Run a cut a few times and it strikes you as clunky **blocks** — a block of speaker A, a block of B, a block of both — and even sequences hitch together like passing **boxcars**. **Boxcar cutting** is cured by returning to how perception works at transitions: **counterpoint** — sound from one shot against image from another — unifies material into a flow. Take a salesman with a great self-image and a lousy technique, filmed both explaining his philosophy over a coffee break (**Sequence A**) and making a pitch to a reluctant customer (**Sequence B**). The conservative, first-assembly method **alternates blocks** — explanation, sales talk, explanation, sales — until it's as predictable as a punching bag. Instead, **interleave with split-edits**: start A explaining, fade up B's aggressive greetings, cut to B (a trapped customer), fade up A again (he's moved uncomfortably close to his junior), cut back to B (the customer backing away angrily). In the overlap areas, **picture from one sequence is counterpointed against sound from the other** — description against practice, ideas against reality — so the audience sees the **irony in the gap** between the man's self-image and his behavior. Talking-head footage is kept minimal, the behavioral evidence predominates, and the material prunes down to a **muscular, spare version that cannot be scripted beforehand.** (Shoot both scenes whole, then make one parallel action for the other.)

## Assessing the rough cut

Each rough cut gets high scrutiny; **deal with the largest-scale problems first**, sharpening to finer points across versions.

| Axis | Interrogation |
|---|---|
| **Exposition** | Too much, too little, or right? Integrated with action, or does the film pause to inform? What could be inferred if removed, or delayed to make the audience wonder? |
| **Momentum & tension** | Is momentum maintained? Is every scene moving the story forward? Is tension sustained — and where does it falter, and why? |
| **Duration** | Which sequences feel long, what material redundant? How does the whole-film length feel? |
| **Characters** | Is each major arc logical and satisfying? Anything misleading or alienating? Is each established *before* becoming dramatically necessary? |
| **Balance** | Does it breathe like music, each movement balanced and inevitable? Is there a satisfying alternation of interiors and exteriors, intensity and release (avoiding unintended claustrophobia)? |
| **Theme & meaning** | What's the present thematic impact, who or what delivers it, and where is the intended impact wayward or failing? |

As each cut nears the mark, questions localize: which sequences want **later in-points / earlier out-points** (enter and leave in movement, not stasis)? Where does each character exert **maximum impact**? Which sequences cry out for rhythm and pacing? **Is every word of dialogue necessary** — could a reaction shot replace words? Does dialogue editing produce tension, flow, and subtext? **How little exposition can you get away with?** How effective is the ending — are there **false endings** before (or after) the true one? Fix only the major faults before the next viewing, then screen for a small audience: **fresh eyes bring new sight when you're fatigued with your own work.**

## The audience actively imagining

By weaving a texture of sound and picture that **invites interpretation**, a film juxtaposes antithetical elements with great economy and kindles the audience's involvement in resolving the story's dialectical tensions — no longer passively submitting, but living a critical inner life (the awareness Brecht sought in theatre). A bored teenager at a store window watches a mother and child argue in the street while an off-screen radio details a **dog show**; we hear breeds and traits over the family quarrel, the argument raised to public spectacle while the naive main character never notices — her unconsciousness and a satirical view of parenthood compressed into a **30-second shot.** That's economy.

**Summary — consciousness probes its surroundings:** **mono-directionally** (eyes and ears on one source) or **bi-directionally** (eyes and ears on different sources), so that **ears can pull eyes forward** to a new setting and **eyes can pull ears forward** — imparting the subjective sensation of a character's shifting planes of consciousness.
