# Chapter 10 — Form and Style

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 10.
> **Scope:** how the controlling POV becomes concrete cinematic **form** across the four aesthetic areas — **visual design, sound design, performance style, editorial style** — plus rhythmic design and the emergent, uncontrollable signature called directorial style.

## Core idea

The screenplay is *content*; **form is how that content appears on the screen** — and form is never merely functional. Audiences know instinctively that **good fiction is not a reproduction of life but an enactment of ideas about it**, so a memorable film carries a form special to its story's purpose and themes. Once you fix the **controlling point of view** and your purpose, the most effective options for every formal element follow: genre, imagery, lenses, camera positions and handling, lighting, set-dressing and wardrobe, performance style, and even the *order* in which events reach the screen. Directors tell stories through four broad areas — **visual design, sound design, performance style, editorial style** — which is exactly why film production teams are built around them.

## Form follows POV

Take a short film about a grocery-store holdup. The first decision is the **controlling POV** — the woman owner, a near-sighted old man buying a lottery ticket, or the young robber — because the robbery means something different to each, and each notices different things. That single choice then suggests everything formal: the **lenses and camera positions** that add up to one person's way of seeing; the **camera handling** (static, handheld, dolly) that carries the director's comment and emotional tone; **lighting** flowing from time of day, mood, and the character's subjective state; particular **set-dressing and wardrobe**; a **performance style** matched to the scene's POV; and finally the **screen order** — the crime shown out of chronology, in remembered fragments, or in conflicting witness versions. Screen order too is subservient to the controlling POV, because the whole film is ultimately filtered through the Storyteller, whose agenda is distinct from any character's.

**The Storyteller's interrogation.** To make form expressive rather than default, force these questions during writing, design, shooting, and editing:

- Who acts, in what way, and *why*?
- Why does this material attract me — does it echo an experience of mine?
- What can this dramatization say about life and living?
- What do I want the audience to see, consider, and wonder about at each phase?
- How do locations, visual aesthetic, and sound design contribute to what I want to express?

## Visual design

**Visual design = art direction + cinematography.**

| **Art direction** | **Cinematography** |
|---|---|
| locations, set dressing, costuming, props, makeup | recording format, lighting, exposure, lens choice, focus, camera angles and movement |

A film gains power when character, story, and theme emerge **behaviourally and visually** rather than theatrically through dialogue. *The Seventh Seal*'s low-key, high-contrast black-and-white and gloomy medieval landscapes prime us for superstition and terror with minimal dialogue; at the opposite pole, *Amélie*'s warm, painterly, sepia-tinged Paris — deep greens and reds, soft light, wide lenses deepening the architecture, sweeping camera moves — is a semi-fantastic city filtered through the heroine's romantic subjectivity. Both are *constructed* worlds whose look flows from POV and purpose.

## Sound design

Sound is roughly half of what makes a film work, and it is a storytelling tool to design **from scripting onward**, not a cosmetic layer added at the mix. Randy Thom's plea to **"screenwrite for sound"** means: let characters *listen*, let sound occasionally lead the picture, and leave quiet spaces that withhold information so momentum channels through sound. Beyond dialogue support, sound can indicate period or a change of time/place, connect unrelated ideas, heighten or diminish ambiguity, startle or soothe, and render objects or actions not visible on screen.

The **components of a sound design**:

| Component | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Speech** | **dialogue** recorded on location (**sync sound**), **ADR** dubbed in post, and **voice-over** narration |
| **Sound effects** | sync effects, **Foley** recorded in post, and library **hard effects** |
| **Ambience / atmospheres** | environmental beds (playground, terminal, restaurant), recorded on location or added from library |
| **Music** | **source music** with a visible in-world source, or **score music** underscoring from outside the story |

Two orthogonal distinctions govern the whole aural universe:

- **Diegetic** — sound from *within* the world we watch (dialogue, footfalls, passing traffic, a car radio).
- **Non-diegetic** — heard by the audience but not the characters; applied authorially, so it *editorialises* — most commonly score music, or omniscient voice-over (which may later be revealed as a character's, as in *The White Ribbon*).

Sound also carries **subjectivity**: *Come and See* switches from the objective roar of explosions to a deafened boy's high-pitched ringing, holding that aural POV until his hearing returns — a discreet way to draw us from an objective soundscape into a character's.

**Psychoacoustics.** A good soundtrack is not measured by quantity or complexity but by the **psychological journey** it leads you on; effective sound is specific and narratively focused, not generic. In *Chernobyl*'s "suicide squad" sequence — three men in masks, in pitch darkness, unable to speak — the emotion is carried almost entirely by the crackling dosimeter, whirring hand-crank flashlights, laboured breathing, and splashing boots, an escalating composition that supplies "the profound scariness" no visible face could.

## Performance style

Performance runs on a sliding scale between two poles:

| **Naturalistic** | **Stylized** |
|---|---|
| the actor inhabits the role so we accept a *real person* (dialect, voice, gesture, posture) | mannerisms deliberately exaggerated or surreal, holding the character at ironic distance |
| *Roma*, *Eighth Grade*, *Boyhood*, *Fruitvale Station* (often mixing pros and non-actors, per the Neo-realists) | *Sorry to Bother You*, *Edward Scissorhands*, *The Big Lebowski* |

Most films fall between the poles. All directors aim for performances **"believable and convincing" within the world of the film**, even when calling attention to themselves — stylizing never needs to sever emotional connection (*Sorry to Bother You*'s Cassius moves us as much as *Roma*'s Cleo).

## Editing style

Editing is a postproduction procedure, but so major an authorial component that its style and rhythm must be anticipated from conceptualization — shooting and editing are best conceived holistically.

| Style | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Continuity style** | multiple angles (**coverage**) edited to construct coherent time, space, and action while rendering the author invisible; invites easy emotional identification. The default, against which others are "non-continuity." |
| **Long-take style** | continuous real-time action in long, unedited shots; immediacy and unbroken emotional threads (*Nine Lives*' ten-minute scenes). Reveals that cuts are authorially intrusive and cost audience energy to decode. |
| **Jump-cut style** | accentuated spatial/temporal disjunctions; self-conscious, "honest" about artifice, edgy energy (*Breathless*). |
| **Associative / intellectual** | overt thematic, emotional, or political links between shots not joined by space or time; the Soviet montage tradition (Eisenstein, Vertov), alive in *Requiem for a Dream*. |

These are not mutually exclusive: film language lets most films work **eclectically**, and even conservative continuity films reach for non-continuity techniques at elevated or revealing moments.

## Rhythmic design

Well-told stories sustain involvement through **variety** — Shakespeare juxtaposes action, monologue, and comedy; long and short scenes; group scenes and soliloquies. "Film is mainly rhythm; it is inhalation and exhalation in continuous sequence" (Bergman). Rhythm emanates at any moment from many sources, and experienced editors and directors judge their combined "orchestration" instinctively:

- shot **length, composition, and movement** in the frame;
- **pacing** of shots within a sequence, and sequences against their neighbours;
- **scene alternation** (night to day);
- **emotional rhythms** (ebb and flow);
- **camera movement**;
- **ambient sound, effects, speech patterns, breathing**;
- **performance pace** (mannerism, temperament, predicament).

## Directorial style

"Style is just the outside of content, and content the inside of style" (Godard) — the two can't be separated. A film's style emerges from the screenplay's dramatic requirements *and* from its makers' identity: a Lynch, del Toro, Loach, Coen, or Jeunet film is instantly recognisable because personality and taste are written all over it. This last, virtually **uncontrollable** element is what is properly called directorial style. You can no more choose it than you can choose your own identity — serve each controllable aspect well, and people will come to recognise something they call your "voice."

## Studio application

- **The four aesthetic areas *are* the crew departments.** Visual design maps to the [Cinematographer](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py) (framing, lens, focus), [Gaffer](../../../sequitur/crew/lighting.py) (lighting), and [Key Grip](../../../sequitur/crew/grip.py) (`CameraMovement`/`MotionSpeed`); sound design to the [speech/TTS layer](../../../sequitur/speech.py) (dialogue, ADR, voice-over) plus a future score/ambience layer; editorial style to the [Editor](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py); performance style to the render prompt built in [`prompt.py`](../../../sequitur/prompt.py). "Form follows POV" is literally the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) reconciling those departments under a single controlling perspective ([storyline 0014](../../../context/storyline/0014-the-crew-behaviour.md)).
- **The editing-style table maps onto `EditCategory`.** *Continuity style* is the assembler's invisible default; *associative/intellectual* editing is precisely `EditCategory.CONCEPT` in [`editorial.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py) ("juxtaposition creates implied meaning not stated in the story") — see [Grammar of the Edit Ch. 6](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch06-transitions-and-edit-categories.md).
- **Rhythmic design is the pacing knob the edit model owns.** "Inhalation and exhalation," shot-length variety, and scene alternation are the energy/tension contour of the [`edit.py`](../../../sequitur/edit.py) timeline executed by [`cutter.py`](../../../sequitur/cutter.py) — pacing as a first-class parameter, not fixed clip lengths.
- **Diegetic vs. non-diegetic is a sound-layer split.** Source music and sync effects sit *inside* the scene; score and omniscient voice-over sit *outside* it and editorialise — a routing distinction the sound layer must encode, and one that (like subjective sound) is a downstream consequence of the POV fixed in [Ch. 9](ch09-cinematic-point-of-view.md).
- **Directorial style is the emergent property of a consistent persona.** The "uncontrollable" signature is what a stable Director [`PersonaJudgment`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py) produces across many productions — style as the *fingerprint* of a persona over grounded role vocabularies, not a tunable field.

Tone crystallises these style areas into the "rules of the world" — carried forward in [Ch. 11 — Tone, Style, and Genre](ch11-tone-style-and-genre.md).
