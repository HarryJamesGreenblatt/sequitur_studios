# Chapter 23 — Planning the Visual Design

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 23.
> **Scope:** how preproduction converts an aesthetic *concept* into concrete, coordinated visual *choices* — the work of the **production designer** (art department) and **cinematographer** (camera department) — plus the questionnaire, lookbook, and breakdown sheet that drive it.

## Core idea

Visual design begins the moment you analyze the screenplay and imagine the film's look; rehearsal then sharpens vague instincts into decidable questions. Preproduction's job is to turn aesthetic principle into **specific instructions** — the exact style of settings, wardrobe, lighting, and camerawork for every scene — because a crew cannot build a coherent world from adjectives. Two principal collaborators execute this with the director: the **production designer** (a.k.a. **art director**), head of the art department, who owns the physical environment (locations, sets, set dressing, **props**, costume, makeup, hair); and the **director of photography** (**DP** / **cinematographer**), head of camera, who owns the photographic look (lighting, composition, angle, format, lens, exposure, focus, and — with the colorist — the grade).

The governing law: **nothing in the frame is neutral — it all "reads."** Every furnishing, garment, hair choice, and shaft of light contributes to mise-en-scène, tone, characterization, and ultimately meaning. Design is coordination: every department is steered by one directorial vision so that location, light, cloth, prop, makeup, season, and weather all express the same point of view on the enclosed world.

## The visual design questionnaire

Work **broad → specific**: first interrogate the whole film, then break down each scene. Bring photos to focus discussion.

**Whole-film questions** — premise and theme(s); mood progressions; genre; characters (age, class, occupation, living situation, self-presentation); the kind of location each scene needs and what each contributes to *scene meaning* and *film premise*; season(s) and their meaning; characters' environments and belongings; props the narrative needs and what they add to character; each character's clothes and what they tell us; the **color palette and progression** across sets/costume/photographic look; and any **ruling visual motif** (gritty realism, primary color, hyperbole, iconic landscape, period accuracy, perilous shadow, etc.).

**Scene-by-scene breakdown** — for each scene, decide: character and color tonality of location/dressing/props; amount and distribution of **light**; color tonality of the photographic look; costume and hairstyle; and how the film's overall motif is reflected here.

## Research and the lookbook

Research first, decide second. Study films of similar genre, setting, or period — then leave the film world for **catalogs, photojournalism, fashion history, painting, and documentary photography**. Directors and DPs routinely cite painters, illustrators, and art-historical movements (Navarro citing Goya and Arthur Rackham for *Pan's Labyrinth*). Do field research: photograph candidate locations, sketch, and collect anything that speaks to the design.

Gather it all into one place — the **lookbook** (physical wall or online board). As it grows, patterns emerge that lead toward the film's ultimate look; the concentrated act of articulation reliably yields fresh resonances back in the script.

## Locations and sets

Student and independent work shoots mostly **on location**, so finding the right location deserves the same rigor as casting — it must serve the script *and* express the Storyteller's point of view. Even a "perfect" real location, especially an interior, usually needs **set dressing**: rearranging, adding/removing furniture and objects, painting, hanging things, fitting window treatments.

**Location scouting** — like casting, means auditioning several candidates. Ideally the director scouts **with the DP and production designer**; if extensive sync-sound dialogue is planned, bring the **sound recordist** too. Evaluate each on four axes:

| Axis | What to weigh |
|---|---|
| **Aesthetics** | Is the look right for the script — and what *else* can it contribute to tone/theme (architecture, backgrounds, light quality, color)? Can it be dressed? |
| **Logistics** | Safe for people/equipment? Power available and sufficient? For natural light, which way do windows face relative to the sun? Big enough for crew/gear/cast? Access? Bathrooms? |
| **Access** | Available for your dates/hours? Terms/rental affordable? Any strings attached — hostile neighbors ready to call police? |
| **Sound** | Ambient noise appropriate and controllable? Acoustics? |

**Building sets** — the right move when the script needs something too specialized to find, or when needs are too simple to justify moving cast and crew. The art department draws plans; on approval, carpenters and painters build (elaborate or cheap). *Occult: The Ghosts of Dunkirk* recreated a Tiger I tank interior from plywood — a viewing-hatch enclosure, tight framing, dark lighting, and atmospheric smoke made it "absolutely convincing." Scale and suggestion beat literal reconstruction on a budget.

## Cinematography

Between preproduction and production the DP and director build the visual interpretation from broad concept down to every composition. Their shared choices:

- General **color palette**
- **Color-grading looks** (and **LUTs**)
- Visual **motifs**
- **Composition** (depth, perspective, treatment of space)
- **Lens** aesthetics
- **Lighting** aesthetics (naturalistic or stylized)
- Shooting and editing strategy per scene
- **Camera movement** (type and method)

The DP is the director's visual "right hand" — a "visually trained composer of images" (Dod Mantle). The balance between technical execution and creative authorship shifts with how visually oriented the director is; the director/DP chemistry is decisive at every budget level. (Coverage strategy and the lens are elaborated in the sibling chapters on the shooting script and cinematography.)

## Wardrobe, makeup, and hairdressing

Treat clothing as a **coded projection** of the owner's status, self-image, and intent — style, fit, wear, and accessory tell us volumes instantly. Decide what mood each character manifests at different moments, how their dress contrasts with others, and whether wardrobe should **evolve** with the character. In *Chernobyl*'s coal-mine scene, the minister's impeccable powder-blue suit against the soot-blackened miners (soot even on their teeth) communicates the ideological gulf in one image — and the miners smearing his suit with dust makes the theme physical.

Makeup and hair likewise read at a glance: the weekly-bingo player, the returning marine, the surf bum, and the news anchor all demand different treatment. The world is richer than imagination — rely on research and real-world observation. **Caution:** don't let actors do their own hair and makeup to save budget; they invariably make themselves look *good*, which is seldom right for the character.

## Case studies in visual design

Three powerful films, three distinct design logics:

| Film | Register | Design strategy |
|---|---|---|
| *The Wrestler* (Aronofsky) | **Naturalism** | PD Grimes builds Randy's world from meticulous locations/dressing (the memorabilia-crammed trailer *is* the character); costume (glitter tights, cheap blond extensions frozen in the '80s). DP Alberti shoots handheld, documentary-style, available/under-light — gaudy light only in ring and strip club. Location as biography (derelict boardwalk = "old broken-down piece of meat"). |
| *Peterloo* (Leigh) | **Naturalism ↔ classical** | Scrupulous period research (candlelit pub scene lit only by candles), yet color is designed to *argue*: workers in spare, greyish-green colorless tones; ruling class in deep greens, glowing reds, gold — clashing violently with the militia's red-and-blue at the massacre. Cruikshank's flat, 2-D satirical prints drove a longer-lens, flatter, "slightly Kurosawa" massacre style. |
| *Sorry to Bother You* (Riley) | **Hyperbole → expressionism** | PD Kisvarday's "beautiful clutter" — puppets, low-tech FX, mismatched patterns. Costume (Govan) is a thrifted "sartorial mashup." **Method in the madness:** palette tracks Cassius's arc — earthy greens/browns/orange when humble → stark white and cool blue as he rises; wardrobe shifts from father's sweater-vests to sharp colorless power-caller suits. |

The lesson across all three: design is a *system* keyed to premise, not decoration — and color **progression** is one of its most potent narrative tools.

## The script breakdown sheet

The production form that logs every mise-en-scène detail a scene needs — atmosphere (rain, fog, smoke), set dressing, hand props, costume, makeup — one sheet per scene so anyone can see its requirements at a glance. The **assistant director** (or production manager) generates the first pass by reading the screenplay and logging every mise-en-scène item mentioned; the **production designer** then double-checks and enriches it as the design evolves (the script's "12-year-old with the demeanor of a Wall Street trader" gets his leather briefcase, blue button-down, striped tie, and smartphone from the art department). The breakdown sheet is the bridge from written scene to buildable world.
