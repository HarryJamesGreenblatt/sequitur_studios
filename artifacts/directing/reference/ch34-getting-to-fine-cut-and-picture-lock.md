# Chapter 34 — Getting to Fine Cut and Picture Lock

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 34.
> **Scope:** the tail of the assemble phase — breaking editing staleness with a flowchart diagnostic and a trial screening, absorbing feedback without losing the film's core, then converging on the fine cut and the inalterable **picture lock** that releases the sound-design team.

## Core idea

After weeks or months of sustained editing, a **debilitating familiarity** sets in: objectivity fails, every version looks the same and too long, and the darlings you can't bear to cut are exactly the ones the film must lose. This staleness afflicts the **hyphenate** director-editor most, but can infect whole crews — so you now call in **outsiders' reactions**. Before you do, prepare with a **flowchart diagnostic** that abstracts the film into something you can see whole. The **trial screening** then tests the film against first-time viewers; you **listen and interpret without explaining**, absorb the feedback cautiously while holding on to your central intentions, and iterate across several rounds and audiences. When no more big changes are needed you have the **fine cut** — edits finessed, transitions and effects decided, sound design begun — and when not another frame needs to change, you reach **picture lock**, the inalterable picture that hands the film to the sound-design team.

## Diagnostics: making a flowchart

Film is a slippery, deceptive medium whose mesmerizing **present-tense detail** blocks any sense of overview — so translate the film into another form (as statisticians turn figures into a graph) to see it differently. A **block diagram** built **from the screen — not from memory, and certainly not from the script** — gives a fresh, detached perspective; the act of diagramming alone brings revelations. Using the storyline-analysis form (Ch. 17), run the film one sequence at a time and:

1. **Box each sequence's content** — characters and main action — and note its **timing**.
2. **Tag-title what each sequence contributes** — exposition, a new setting/character/mood, or a character whose function develops later.
3. **Examine the whole.** Sequences are not self-contained soloists but the **delayed entries of a choral work** — each new voice cross-modulates with those before, and some foreshadow references that only resolve later.
4. **Draw and annotate the relationships** between each sequence and its neighbors — revealing, for instance, that in **parallel storytelling** one strand has drifted too far from its counterpart.
5. **Rate each sequence's length against its contribution**, and give it an **audience-impact rating** to track how dramatic pressure evolves.

Diagramming forces you to acknowledge what is actually on screen and translate amorphous sensation into hard-edged statement. Common ailments it surfaces:

| Ailment | Cure |
|---|---|
| **Lacks early impact / pedestrian opening** (a late developer) | Try jumping into the story several scenes later; if only setup is lost, take the leap. |
| **Main issues unclear or slow to emerge** | Reposition a scene earlier — even ahead of the titles — to commit the film to an interesting line. |
| **Impact poorly distributed** (dramatic feast or famine) | Redistribute the type and frequency of impact across the length. |
| **Erratic delivery of information** | Fix omissions, redundancy, exposition placed too early (and forgotten) or too late (or too thin). |
| **Same dramatic statement made several ways** (three scenes prove the hero's temper) | Choose the best; reposition or dump the others. |
| **A favorite sequence/character fails to contribute** | Another darling — close your eyes and swing the axe. |
| **Resolution emerges early** → the rest feels inevitable | Rebalance or withdraw indicators to keep the outcome in doubt so the audience stays working. |
| **The film appears to end before it does** | False or multiple endings — a common fault. |

**Naming each ailment leads to its cure.** Implementing a remedy is like resetting a boat's sail — everything looks the same, yet she surges under new power. And knowing what every brick upholds prepares you to test the film's intentions in the trial screening.

## The rough-cut trial screening

The **trial audience** is a half-dozen people whose tastes you respect, giving a **fresh, first-time perspective** — so exclude anyone who knows the project, above all cast, crew, and screenwriter (**the less they know, the better**). Choose people **fluent in the critique-and-feedback process** who can make allowances for a work in progress, because the uninitiated read a rough cut's rawness — provisional soundtrack, inconsistent color and exposure, choppy continuity — as *mistakes*. Ideal viewers are peers (editors, directors, producers, programmers, professors) but also any artist who understands critiquing drafts. Preparation:

- Give the movie a **working title** — part of the viewer's "contract," signaling the story's purpose and identity.
- **Check and adjust sound levels** in software; inaudible or overbearing sound produces misleadingly negative responses even from professionals.
- **Warn** that the film is a work in progress and technically raw, and **call out** important missing elements (music, sound, visual effects) as they arrive.

### Listen closely, guide discussion, do not explain

Your job is to **listen and interpret what people get from the screen.**

- Ask for **impressions of the film as a whole.**
- **Keep quiet and take notes** — the film must stand or fall on its merits.
- **Avoid the temptation to explain.** Filling in missing information or stating your intentions only confuses and compromises their perceptions, and implies they're inept viewers.
- **Focus and direct** their attention, or the discussion drifts from your needs.

Guide the inquiry from large issues toward component parts: *What is the story really about? What are the major issues? Did it feel the right length, or too long? Were any parts unclear or puzzling?* (then list what you suspect made no impression — audiences forget what passed over their heads) *Which parts felt slow? Which were moving or successful? What did you feel about ___ (character)? What did you end up knowing about ___ (situation)?* Targeted inquiry lets you **test the function you assigned each sequence.** When someone insists on discussing the film *they* would have made, diplomatically redirect.

## Post-screening reflection

Absorbing criticism is emotionally draining — it is usual to feel threatened, slighted, and misunderstood, and to leave with a raging headache — so an **audio recording** lets you revisit the feedback later in peace.

- **Balancing different views** — absorb multiple impressions, then guess how viewers arrived at them; watching with their eyes, a single extra line or inserted close-up often solves a problem without compromising the film. **Weigh how many people report a difficulty**, allow for individual subjectivity and acuity, and note that opposing comments may cancel out — but comments **clustering** around one area, even in disagreement, **raise a red flag.**
- **Make changes cautiously** — change nothing substantial without careful reflection; asking for criticism invites people to make their mark, and **you can never please everyone.**
- **Hold on to your central intentions** — never let the **core idea** of the film get lost, and act only on suggestions that support and further it. This is a dangerous phase for any artist: let go of the work's underlying identity and you lose your direction. Don't carve into the film precipitously; it may take a week for contradictory passions to settle.
- **The uses of procrastination** — pleased or depressed, **stop working for a few days or weeks** and do something different; on return, the problems and their solutions no longer seem overwhelming. Such anxiety is the long, painful labor before birth.

Expect **several rounds** of alterations tried on **several new audiences** — unconscionably slow to the novice — and you may re-show the final cut to the original trial audience to see what changes they report. Films genuinely **evolve in editing**; a year of part-time work on a 30-minute film is nothing unusual.

## The fine cut

When your scenes have the placement and dynamism you sought and you sense no more big changes are needed, you have reached the **fine cut**, whose three principal tasks are:

1. **Finesse every edit, one by one** — trimming or adding a few frames to sharpen a reaction's timing or land a cut on action perfectly.
2. **Decide transitions and effects** — editor and director settle the type and duration of every dissolve, fade, and visual effect.
3. **Begin the sound design** — the editor and postproduction sound team build the multi-track design one layer at a time: **cleaning dialogue** of unwanted noise, **constructing and smoothing ambient** backgrounds for every scene, and **laying music and building sound-effects tracks** — the editor tweaking edits throughout to keep everything in sync.

### Knowing when to stop

**Avoid setting deadlines**; instead look for compelling evidence that development has come to a **standstill.** Some directors fiddle beyond usefulness and even spoil their work, justifying the compulsion as "perfectionism" when it is really the **fear of letting go** — like refusing to admit the child is now an adult.

## Picture lock

When every creative editing decision is made and **not another frame needs to change, you have picture lock.** The picture is now deemed **inalterable**, and the sound-design team goes to work adding music, placing effects, and layering tracks in perfect sync — one more opportunity for the director to shape the film's **tone and authorial point of view** through sound.
