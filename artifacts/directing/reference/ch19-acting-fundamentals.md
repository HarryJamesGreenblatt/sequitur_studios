# Chapter 19 — Acting Fundamentals

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 19.
> **Scope:** the craft of screen acting a director must understand to lead a cast — belief, interior life, playable objectives, and how film acting differs from theatre. Grounds the studio's **actor / performance** layer.

## Core idea

Photography's innate realism makes audiences demand **psychological realism** from actors, so a director must understand acting well enough to become something of a drama coach. Nearly all modern approaches descend from **Konstantin Stanislavsky**, who distinguished actors who **grip** an audience from those trading in empty gesture, and traced the difference to a few psychological truths that recur in life as well as on screen. The master principle: an actor cannot *will* an emotion or a result — they can only pursue what is **natural to the character**, and the truth (and feeling) follows.

## Believing it: focus, relaxation, interior monologue

The **prime enemy is losing belief** in what you're doing. Recall how **focused, relaxed, and boundlessly energetic** you feel absorbed in something you love — watched or ignored, you simply *are*. That fusion of **preoccupation + relaxation** silences the internal censor. Its opposite is the room full of strangers (now add a camera) where you feel awkward, rigid, wordless — the film actor's daily reality. These two poles are **the heaven and hell of acting**.

The bridge is Stanislavsky's **magic "if"**: imagining *as if* the circumstances were real starts the imagination and lets the actor relax into the character's **"now"** — really thinking, planning, remembering, deciding (the fascinating, familiar actions of a living person, effortless in documentary subjects because they're genuinely absorbed). Many actors sustain an **interior monologue** — talking themselves through the character's moment — which holds focus across the many takes and angles a scene demands; asking actors to **externalize** it in rehearsal lets the director locate meaning and subtext. **Losing focus** is the occupational hazard (drowning is to the diver): a misplaced prop, a wrong line, an eyeline distraction, or even the *fear* of losing focus, and a believable character crumbles into a beleaguered actor — the camera registers the lapse instantly. Trained actors **regain focus** reflexively by fixing on something real in the character's here-and-now (a carpet pattern, a sleeve's texture), then broadening back out — righting the craft instead of drowning. The director's part is the belief work done **before the camera rolls**: an actor must not merely understand a line but **believe in and live** it, and will tell you when a line or move pulls them out of character — *listen*.

## Interior life & playable actions

Acting **starts from the imagination** creating another person's reality (easy as children, harder once self-conscious). Truth comes from an authentic **interior life**, disciplined attention, and **acting on** fellow actors while allowing oneself to be **acted upon** — the anxious actor instead cocoons in preparation, visualizing the script page and performing premeditated, mechanical actions. The director's job is an eye for that inauthenticity and techniques to restore the stream of inner life.

**Justifying the givens.** Every action and line is justified by the **givens** — all information given or implied by the script: background, backstory, recent experience, needs, plot goals, relationships. Where playing goes fuzzy or unmotivated, probe the actor's understanding and remedy it.

**Objectives in the active voice.** Characters grip us when they **actively pursue needs** — even a "passive" lead pursues goals (ones that shield him from acting). Objectives are always **transitive verbs**, consciously constructed by breaking the part into playable actions. A well-formed objective has three parts:

| Component | Example |
|---|---|
| **Active verb** | "I will **charm** …" |
| **Someone/something acted upon** | "… Marie, my brother's standoffish wife" |
| **Desired, measurable outcome** | "… so she asks me to stay to dinner" |

You **cannot act "being angry"** — it's abstract and unplayable; nor a **negative** intention ("I don't want to be here when he drinks") — it must be made active ("I will leave the room to show him I hate his drinking"). Objectives give every moment shape and make a character fascinating to play against.

**Scene beats.** Actors break each scene into patterns of **action / objective / outcome** — **scene beats**. These overlap the screenplay's dramatic beats but aren't identical: a revealing shot, a visual juxtaposition, or a narrative detail the *viewer* knows but the *character* doesn't can create dramatic beats in **cinematic** rather than performance terms — which is why "beat" is a slippery, dual term.

**Developing interior life.** Scene by scene the actor decides what the character wants, thinks, notices, remembers, imagines — and, from experience of life, exactly how this character walks, sits, eats, cries out. **No action is ever "in general"**: each is unique to this character and this mood, emitting emotionally precise clues. Other players feed this — every relationship evokes a **different "me,"** so a character shows a range of selves (tender to a son, hard to an abuser); real people are complex and seemingly contradictory.

## Business, personalizing, and the mind–body connection

**Business** — the small physical activities that involve the body and reveal inner reality — can be entrancing (Colin Firth's Bertie absently assembling a model plane in *The King's Speech*, becoming a vulnerable boy as he discloses his childhood). Because acting is *doing*, business is named in **verbs** (*lowering* the eyes, *searching* a pocket); **interior** actions matter as much as exterior. Actors **personalize** by speaking of the character in the **first person** ("*I'm* getting agitated," not "*she's* getting depressed") — taking ownership of the here-and-now.

The **mind–body connection**: as Stanislavsky held, **no inner state exists without outward evidence** in face and body. An actor genuinely experiencing the character's thoughts expresses them unconsciously through the **physical instrument** (body, face, action, voice) — so even **interior actions** (choosing, holding back, calculating) become visible and interesting, which is why the best actors stay **alive during reaction shots** while mediocre ones go dead and fall to **indicating** (signaling thought instead of experiencing it).

## Verbal action, subtext, obstacle, communion

**Verbal action** — dialogue is action: speech used to *act on* someone to gain or change something. Lecter's "You know Florence?" isn't a question but a move to belittle Clarice and seize the upper hand; in a truly cinematic script **almost every word is a verbal action**. **Subtext** — the real meaning beneath the line ("So, shall I see you again?" carrying "maybe I can get you to repay me," or "do you still care?"); actors derive subtext from the overall objectives, then decide how the character handles each moment (nearly every scene of *The Social Network* runs on it — Parker's pitch isn't about "a billion dollars" but about **"what's cool"**). **Obstacle & adapting** — goals meet obstacles (external: a locked door; internal: fear of the boss), and *how* a character adapts reveals them; adaptation between actors is like two people balancing in a small boat, and the script says nothing of it because **creating the adaptations is the actors' responsibility** (they suggest; the director accepts, modifies, or asks for a new solution). **Anticipating** — knowing what's coming, the actor pre-reacts; relaxation means giving up looking ahead ("never mind the lines, just play the scene"). **Communion vs. isolation** — an actor cocooned in preparation acts in **isolation**, deaf to the query in another's voice; when everyone reacts to each other's shifting in-the-moment nuances, the scene's **hidden life** emerges and stays alive across ten takes — that live inter-character exchange is **communion**.

## Drawing on personal experience

**Sense memory & emotional memory.** Our bank of sensory memories (baked potatoes at home, rope in a gym, a skylark's song) evokes time, place, mood, people; an actor correlates the character's experiences with analogous memories. The extraordinary reflex: **the actions themselves evoke the emotion**. Cover your mouth exactly as you do after speaking out of turn and you feel real embarrassment; try to feel embarrassed with **no** movement and you can't — **we cannot choose to feel an emotion**. Most people think emotion produces action; **in acting it's the reverse** — authentic chosen actions **release** authentic feeling in the actor, *every* time they're performed, moving the audience every time. Actors reach the emotional through the **sensory** (Proust's madeleine unlocking childhood).

**Using the actor's own emotion as the character's.** When something real intrudes (a headache, a partner's surprise move, a misplaced prop), the trained actor **co-opts** it into the character's present rather than fighting it (Second City trains comedians to sustain character under any condition). Kept this busy on so many levels, the actor stops worrying about lines or being watched — everything outside the character's subjective sphere recedes (Rod Steiger holding character *and* a German accent even off-camera in *Across the Bridge*).

## Theatre vs. film acting

Some theatre performances ring **false on screen** — a psychological matter of **where the actor draws support**. The **theatre actor** works symbiotically with the audience's pulse and enlarges voice and action for distance; the **film actor** has **no audience but the director** and must work wholly from what the character thinks, feels, and does, since the camera captures every eyebrow twitch. Crew must stay **remote and impersonal** — the moment actors play for their approval, you have a theatrical performance on film. First-time screen work leaves even trained actors feeling **alone and self-doubting**; it is the director's job to draw them into an **intimate, internalized** way of sustaining belief — and to re-calibrate for a cast that mixes trained, untrained, veteran, and first-timer so performances stay **stylistically consistent** with the film's tone.

## Studio application

- **This chapter grounds a performance/actor layer that NO current code and NO other source models** — the emotional-intent tier that would sit *above* the render backends and drive them. "Directing actors" in a generative studio means encoding **playable intent** (objective, subtext, business, beat) as structured input to the video/voice renderers, not merely a text prompt. It is the missing seam between the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py)'s briefs and what a shot *feels* like.
- **Objectives, subtext, and beats are structured fields, not prose.** The active-voice objective (**verb → target → outcome**) is a three-field record — the performance analogue of how a [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py) bundles independent grammar fields and how a [`Brief`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) carries `mood`/`hints`. A future **`Actor`/performance concept** would attach such a record to each character-in-scene and pass it into [`prompt.py`](../../../sequitur/prompt.py) so the render carries intent, not just blocking. **Scene beats** are the shared unit with the Editor: they overlap the screenplay's dramatic beats but add performance strategy the post layer ([`edit.py`](../../../sequitur/edit.py)) must honor.
- **"Sensory → emotional" and "no inner state without outward evidence" are the generative levers.** A model cannot be told to *feel*; it can be given the **actions and sensory business** that read as feeling — the exact inversion the chapter describes. Casting a character's look/voice ([`ImageStudio`](../../../sequitur/image.py), [`SpeechRenderer`](../../../sequitur/speech.py)) supplies the **physical instrument**; the performance layer supplies the **playable action** that instrument executes. Voice carries subtext directly, tying into [storyline 0011](../../../context/storyline/0011-the-voice-layer.md), Rose's [voice-over](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch09-the-voice-over.md) / [voice-editing](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch13-editing-voices.md) craft, and the Taxonomy's [Point of View](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch07-point-of-view.md) layer (whose interiority the actor makes visible).
- **The director as the actor's sole audience maps onto the Director-as-reconciler.** With no live audience, the film actor's belief is held by the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py); the generative parallel is that consistency and emotional truth are the Director's to reconcile across shots, and the swappable [`Judgment`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py) (heuristic **A** → persona **B** → human) is where "coaching" would live — a *PersonaJudgment* reasoning about objective and subtext, a **Producer** (HITL) overriding when a take rings false. Keeping the character in **focus while merely thinking or listening** is precisely the reaction-shot problem the render must solve.
