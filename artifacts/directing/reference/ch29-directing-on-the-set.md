# Chapter 29 — Directing on the Set

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 29.
> **Scope:** the director's craft *during the shoot* — leading the cast, dividing attention between actors and crew, running the take cycle, evaluating each setup, keeping morale and momentum, and wrapping the day. This is the human engine of the SHOOT phase.

## Core idea

On set the director is a **conductor** whose instrumentalists are the actors, and the actors' respect rests on one thing: how accurately and briefly you can reflect **what each just did** and where **in detail** each should aim next. Your major responsibility is *always* to the cast. Inside you may feel like a fraud, but you play the role of the confident, all-caring parent figure because the whole unit needs to believe it — **fake it till you make it**. Limit the ground you personally oversee by being better prepared than anyone else and by keeping everyone busy. Keep the roles of **friend and director separate**: a friend forgives; a director upholds the purpose of a project that carries many people's work and much money. Under stress, aim to be **respected rather than liked** — admiration and liking arrive later, once things turn out well.

Read across the studio's own architecture, this chapter grounds the **SHOOT-phase Director** — `engine.run(Phase.SHOOT)` — as the on-set reconciler who keeps the crew's contributions and the (abstracted) performance aimed at a single intention.

## Leading the cast through anxiety

Whatever level was reached in rehearsal now goes to the test, and actors feel they are going over Niagara in a barrel. Schedule the **least demanding material first** as a warm-up, because jitters peak and confidence bottoms out just before first shooting. Take each actor aside and say something **sincere, supportive, and private** — it founds a special understanding that reaches the audience through the film, for whom you are currently the surrogate.

- **Expect displaced tension** in the first day or two. Fear of failing makes normal, often shy people pick quarrels or belittle the work; it is human, not childishness. Public recognition of even small achievements, plus the crew's astute diplomacy, restores morale.
- **Warn them filming is s-l-o-w.** Even a professional feature unit shoots only **one to four minutes of screen time per eight-hour day**. Tell actors to bring books, cards, puzzles — anything for the waits between setups.
- **Anxiety subsides into rhythm.** As the cast falls in with the pace, each takes pride in being one of a team, and performances improve enough that you may doubt the usability of early material.

## Dividing yourself between crew and cast

A beginning director wants to personally monitor everything the untried crew does. **Desist.** You will not direct the cast adequately unless you **let the DP and AD lead the crew**. Know what you need from your cast and work unceasingly to get it — that will take all your attention and then some.

## Directing actors through the take cycle

The shoot offers repeated windows to rehearse and re-aim the actors:

| Window | What the director does |
|---|---|
| **Tech rehearsals** | Mostly for the crew, but the cast learns the location — help them **inhabit and exploit the space**, its sets, furniture, and handled objects (decide *where* the forks live). Tweak blocking; give the location reality. |
| **Lighting setups** | While the crew builds the next angle, take each actor aside and **cue his character's recent past and emotional state** on entering. Only you know the prior scene's content (perhaps not yet shot, or shot long ago). This informs *and* motivates; refresh it before every setup, sometimes every take. |
| **Walk-throughs** | With lights, camera, and full crew present, get the actors into the **emotional space** of the scene. Keep complex scenes at **half intensity** until the crew is fully ready — don't squeeze out the freshness while the AC sets focus marks and the boom op finds placement. Run-throughs tell you when a take will "nail it"; that's when you roll. |
| **"Action!"** | The word can be spoken 50 ways — urgent, gentle, questioning, abrasive, fearful. **"Action!" can be a directing cue all by itself.** |
| **Between takes** | The instant you call "Cut!", decide **keeper or another**. From this the actors read whether they were on target. If lacking, clarify what you want next — a framing/lighting fix, or brief, **actable adjustments and goals**. Be clear about what worked and what didn't, but keep it quick and nonjudgmental: **momentum is everything; avoid unnecessary discussion.** |

## Changing goals, not repeating results

New goals may demand something different from each actor in a group scene — the same good level from one, a different shading or energy from another. **Avoid asking anyone for "the same thing again."** The actor is inside his instrument and can't see what you liked; asked to reproduce a result, part of him thinks *"if she liked it, why do it again?"* Give even satisfactory players fresh **objectives that keep them building** rather than repeating.

When an actor asks for another take, decide whether it's warranted — your competency is now under scrutiny (either he saw something you missed, or he's inflating it). The cast should always be allowed to improve, but a serial "just one more" may be an **anxiety fetish** or an attempt to manipulate directorial decisions. If you were fully attentive and saw nothing wrong, say the take was fine and move on; if he convinces you real magic awaits, grant it. One or two episodes reveal whether an actor is sincere or playing a head game.

## Demands, feedback, and the sagging scene

The director's Achilles heel is a **passive, gullible tendency** to accept whatever actors give as their best — you feel guilty that all these people are working for little old you, and you want to be liked. Resist it. Adopt the confident artist's **creative dissatisfaction**: treat each scene as a semblance hiding deeper layers only greater skill can reveal.

- **Never grade players "good" to "bad."** Tell each, in a few words, *what they communicated* (it varies take to take); say what you'd like next; where possible give separate input to individuals. Discreetly, the **script supervisor** — who also watches performance shadings — can confirm your impressions, but ultimately **trust your own instincts**, and clear the mental space for them to operate.
- **Don't *try* to feel** what you should feel naturally — that comes from guilt and blinds you. React like an unattached audience member: if it works, it works; if it doesn't, ask why (fatigue? repetition?) and tell the cast diplomatically. Often an actor knows what went wrong — a rough line, a bit of blocking — so **listen**.
- **A scene sagging into "comfortable middle age"** means the cast is on autopilot, not listening to each other. Remind them to listen, or privately seed a small significant change in one player that impacts the others — build in **little stresses and incompatibilities** so they work off each other and tension returns.

## Side coaching and reaction shots

When a scene goes static and premeditated, use **side coaching**: interpolate, at a quiet moment, a verbal suggestion into the character — *"Terry, she's asking the impossible — she's laying a trap."* Your voice injects a new interior process and the scene moves in a new direction. Introduce this in **rehearsal** and warn actors not to break character, or the surprise ruins it. It is most useful for **reaction shots**: feed a verbal image for the character to see or consider and you get an immediacy of reaction (the countess watching an off-screen swordfight, narrated live, MOS).

- When shooting separate reactions, **avoid having the script supervisor feed lines** — the best reactions are to something *actual*, so keep the other actor performing off-camera even for a whispered overheard conversation. Only for a simple look-and-react (a visitor through a window) is imagination plus side coaching enough.
- **Reaction shots let the audience cocreate a character's inner life** and provide the legitimate cutaways that let you combine the best takes in editing.
- **Never dismiss cast and crew without first covering all likely reactions, cutaways, and inserts for the scene.**

## For the new shot, and challenging the cast

As soon as you have a keeper, **brief the DP about the next shot, then turn to the cast** — brief positive feedback on the last, preparation for the next. The AD may rest the actors while the crew roars into the reset; the cast slips from royalty to obscurity while the equipment gets the attention (no wonder some prefer theatre). Because the medium is **allusive** — audiences expect metaphorical and metaphysical overtones — you must challenge actors in a hundred ways so we see poetry and conflict beneath the surface. Reinforce their own sense of being capable of something better; expect an undertow of complaining and **regard grumbling as the noise in the rigging of a ship pushed to capacity.** Don't imagine you can make everyone feel good.

## Everyone needs feedback

Actors are schooled to depend on **audience feedback**, and now you are their only audience — yet during a take you cannot signal approval. When the camera stops, your job is to briefly give each actor the **sense of closure** a live audience used to provide. Actors are not fooled by empty gestures, so while your brain runs out of control factoring editing possibilities, you must still say something intelligent to trusting, necessarily self-absorbed players. And the moment you do, the crew needs you, the actors ask "what next?", and the PM is at your elbow about next week's warehouse — the glamour of directing turns out to be answering an avalanche of questions on no sleep.

## Handling criticism and friction

- **From the cast:** preferences and criticisms from rehearsal surface more vehemently under duress — favorite scenes, hated scenes, odious characteristics, lines an actor fixates on. In serious cases you may permit **alternative wording**, but only after all other remedies fail and only as a **one-time concession** (or everyone starts rewriting). Most things needn't be decided on the spot: take them in, think, delay changes until sure. Remaining open-minded is **not** swinging like a weather vane; deflect impractical suggestions by being so well-prepared and full of interesting demands that no one has time to carp — without deterring genuinely constructive ideas.
- **From the crew:** a crew member who lets others know how much better they could direct is **intolerable and must be immediately squelched** — nothing diminishes authority faster than a cast feeling directed by committee. **Directing a film is not, has not been, and never will be democratic.** Demarcate all territories before shooting; anyone who strays is told privately and firmly to tend his own area; legitimate complaints go through the DP. When a necessary but unpopular decision is needed, make it, bite the bullet, **do not apologize** — it is usually the one everyone knows is right.

## Directing the crew

Cede control to your senior production personnel, who must stay fully aware and take initiative without waiting to be told. The most important things a director does for the crew:

1. **Communicate the concept, story, and purpose** — show them why you love this story (your investment sets the bar).
2. Have a **thorough shooting script and floorplan** ready, clearly indicating coverage for each scene.
3. Communicate any **blocking/lighting requirement or change precisely**.
4. Don't make **frivolous changes** or unreasonable demands.
5. Bring **energy, mission, and passion** to the set.
6. **Listen to suggestions** — all filtered through the DP or AD.
7. **Respect their expertise** and let them do their job.

**Communicating well:** each crew member should have read and questioned the script and contributed in their specialty; you, in turn, must know the rudiments of each craft and speak its terms. The crew needs **positive, concise directions with maximum advance warning** — technical teams won't rise to genuine crises if foreseeable things went unattended. Avoid **thinking out loud** as the pace heats up; arrive at conclusions and issue brief, unambiguous instructions, and have people repeat any complex instruction (without condescension). Put in writing anything that needs to be. The **AD and DP handle all production/technical questions** so you can answer the needs of the actors and build the film's dramatic content; the **script supervisor** is a key ally, though script supervision's fierce focus on words, actions, and materials means they can't always judge performance quality — so **confer over coverage, especially when you change it.**

## Evaluating the shot

When a new setup is ready you **must carefully check framing and mise-en-scène** on a field monitor (or through the viewfinder). With character or camera movement, watch **every visual element from beginning to end** as framing, composition, and focus shift. **Handheld** poses a unique problem — the camera is untethered and only the operator truly sees what's recorded — so agree with your operator on **beginning/ending compositions, movement speed, choreography with the actors, angle and focus shifts, and image size**; walk the actors (or stand-ins) through, freezing at salient points, and lay **floor marks** for actors and camera so everyone hits their marks. Precision separates the experienced from the inexperienced, but imposing too much control on a green ensemble can wreck morale. Because framing, composition, lighting, and sound coverage are the **formal structuring that translates a live world into cinema**, the director must keep the strongest possible contact with the screen outcome.

> **Digital caution:** watching the whole take on a high-resolution field monitor tells you immediately what you have — but habitual reviewing consumes time, and constantly retreating to the monitor makes actors feel **abandoned** because you're not beside the camera and physically present for them.

## Momentum, dailies, and morale

- **Making progress:** shooting is stop-start, and a crew easily stalls while everyone waits for the elusive "A.N. Other." A good **AD is a sheepdog** hunting bottlenecks and barking everyone into action; during the dips the director's job is to **keep the cast motivated and focused**.
- **When you and the crew are alone:** with a small intimate crew, encourage them (actors elsewhere) to share impressions. Grips, electricians, and ADs work *before* the take and then observe, so their **audience-like reaction** can complement your goal-driven view. Camera and sound are too locally absorbed to register the dramatic reliably, so expect a mixed bag — encourage all views but neither act on nor rebuff implied criticism reflexively. **If most of the crew fault a main character, take serious notice.**
- **Wrapping for the day:** thank each actor **personally** and the crew **collectively** — respectful appreciation says you take nobody for granted and, by implication, demands the same respect back. No wrap is complete without the **AD and UPM reiterating the next day's arrangements**; for exteriors, keep abreast of weather and ready **bad-weather contingencies**. Issue **call sheets**; return rented gear, charge batteries, deliver dailies.
- **Screening dailies:** **dailies** are the raw, unedited (usually sync-sound) footage, reviewed after wrap so problems can be caught before leaving the location. The **crew must see** each day's work; the **cast must not** — actors hate what they see, begin self-directing to cure perceived faults, and turn perfectly good footage into a struggle with their demons. Tell them it's standard practice and promise a first-cut viewing later, once their performances are safely given.
- **Morale, fatigue, intensity:** crew and cast morale interlock; credit and attention to each member sustain loyalty. Fatigue unravels immature personalities and makes work sloppy — **overworking the crew is the key risk**. Guard **creature comforts** (warm, dry, fed, watered, toilets, somewhere to sit) and **avoid working more than four hours without a break**, even a ten-minute coffee. From these primal attentions, people infer the production cares — and most, when valued, go to the ends of the earth for you.
- **You as role model:** your seriousness and intensity set the tone. Sloppy and laid-back invites others to outdo you; demanding much of yourself and others while staying appreciative and welcoming appropriate humor runs a tight ship. **Convert every negative criticism into a positive request for an alternative.** People follow an organized visionary anywhere.
