# Chapter 28 — On Set: Production Begins

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 28.
> **Scope:** the on-set chronology of the shoot — from **load-in through the first shot, the take ritual, slating and logging, coverage, and the strike** — the phase where the plan is dispatched and executed.

## Core idea

Preproduction front-loads the work; the **shoot executes it under intense compression** — so many people, resources, and money converge that everything must be prepared. The director is an **orchestra conductor**, holding many people's work in mind at once, starting from a **detailed movie in the head** and maneuvering cast and crew to realize each part — while obstacles and opportunities force that inner movie to constantly evolve. When the crew turns to you after a shot asking *"Did you get what you wanted?"*, you must answer, and lead. **Crib notes** on index cards (beats, story points) keep intentions from evaporating in the hurly-burly and fatigue of the set.

For the studio, this chapter grounds the **SHOOT-phase dispatch**: `engine.run(Phase.SHOOT, brief)` filters the crew on call, collects each role's contribution, and reconciles it — the code analogue of the on-set choreography and the per-role attention during a take.

## Daily organization

A smoothly running unit signals professionalism (vital when people are low-paid or unpaid). Every shooting day the unit provides:

- A **contact list** (all cell numbers) for emergencies
- Printed **call sheets** well in advance (map, emergency info)
- The day's **scene and shot list** from the schedule
- **Coverage** worked out with the DP and script supervisor
- **Floorplans** for the camera crew, and a **lighting design** the DP derived from them
- **Props and costumes** ready
- The **AD carrying every list**, so nothing lives only in the director's head

## Getting to the first shot: chronology

| # | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Prepare the set** | Art department arrives first — moves/brings furniture, paints, dresses, sets props — usually one location ahead of the unit. |
| 2 | **Load in** | Camera and sound bring gear to the **staging area**: out of the way, convenient, secure. |
| 3 | **Equipment set-up** | Unpack and test everything; AC enters settings (format, ISO, profile) and white-balances; DIT calibrates the field monitor and grading system; the **backup station** and written protocol are established. |
| 4 | **Rig the stage** | Camera and electricians rough in camera placement and lights from the floorplans/lighting design (**wider masters first**); art stands by for tweaks; sound works out miking. |
| 5 | **First line-up** | Crew asks the director for the precise setup; director confirms framing and in/out-of-shot with the DP; operator frames the expected action. |
| 6 | **Blocking & tech rehearsal** | Actors walk through the real space; final blocking set; **floor and lens marks** placed; director and DP review framing and lighting at each stage. |
| 7 | **Sound + final lighting** | Sound studies light placement and facing directions, plans coverage, may request blocking/lighting compromises. Actors go to wardrobe/makeup while the crew sets **final lighting** — slow work with multiple keys — using **stand-ins** of similar build. |
| 8 | **Wardrobe, makeup** | Cast into costume and makeup; sound plants **wireless mics** as they dress. |
| 9 | **Rehearsals** | Director, cast, and script supervisor do last-minute work; a missing actor's lines are **run** by the script supervisor. |
| 10 | **Dress rehearsals** | Walk-through again for cast, DP, operator, sound; hair/makeup touch-ups; a **dry run** in costume without the camera (low intensity to conserve energy); costume problems surface; sound settles levels and boom positions; script supervisor takes a **scene timing**. |
| 11 | **Take the shot!** | When all feel ready, roll camera. |

The first shots always take an eternity to light; the **AD applies judicious pressure** and reports readiness. **Schedule a light first day** so the unit finds its rhythm on schedule and feels energized.

## Roll camera

As it comes time to roll: the **script supervisor** checks costumes, props, and that nothing is misplaced; the **AD** marshals starting positions, seals doors/windows against exterior sound, and calls for silence; a last hair/makeup check; the DP confirms lights; on film, the AC checks the gate. Distances are measured, marks set, and the focus puller is ready. The script supervisor confers with the AC on the next **slate number** — every shot must be correctly slated and recorded in the camera, sound, and continuity logs, or the footage can't be organized in the cutting room.

### The slate

A **slate** (clapper board) is a dry-erase board with a hinged bar; banging it closed creates a matched **visual + aural sync point.** The **slate loader** (usually 2nd AC) updates and operates it. Three functions:

- **Visual** — identifies production, scene, and take at the head of the shot (may carry a color chip chart for grading).
- **Aural** — the spoken scene/take number identifies the shot on the soundtrack.
- **Sync** — the closing bar gives an exact frame to align the *bang!* against — vital for **double system** (picture and sound recorded separately). A **smart slate** displays timecode that freezes on the clap; camera and recorder must be **jam-synced** each morning as their clocks drift. **Single system** (sound and picture on one medium) needs no aural clapper — but you must still identify every shot/take visually before "Action."

### Numbering methods

| Method | System | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Scene/shot/take** | Numbers from the script scene: "Scene 14-C, Take 3." | Big, highly supervised Hollywood productions; overkill (and fragile) for small units. |
| **2 — Cumulative setup/take** | Begins at Slate 1; each new setup gets the next number, Take 1 ("Slate 142, Take 2" = 142nd setup, 2nd take). | Documentaries and overstretched small crews — no script liaison, no fluster when the plan departs. |

### Shooting logs

| Log | Kept by | Records |
|---|---|---|
| **Camera log** | AC | Each memory card's content by shot/take/timing; each card's **roll number**; f-stop, lens, ISO, filtration. |
| **Sound log** | recordist | Scene/take numbers and whether each track is **sync or wild**. |

A quiet extra function: logging **which serial-numbered machine** made each recording, so a hum or glitch can be traced and the offending unit pulled.

## Calling a shot: from "Quiet" to "Action"

An unvarying ritual gets everything rolling:

1. **AD:** *"Quiet — we're about to roll"* / *"Picture's up!"* Silence descends; the 2nd AC holds the slate in frame.
2. **AD:** *"Roll sound."*
3. **Sound Mixer** (after a ~5-second pre-roll): *"Sound speed."*
4. **Camera Operator** (rolls camera): *"Mark it."*
5. **2nd AC:** *"14-C, Take 1"* — **BANG!** — then scuttles out of frame.
6. **Director:** *"Action."* (said to convey the mood wanted); the script supervisor starts the stopwatch.

Scripted cues (a car horn, an off-screen call, a passing plane) are called out by the **script supervisor** at the right beat so the actors respond.

**Starting without a slate.** When the setup is too tight or the material spontaneous, signal camera/sound to roll quietly; after the action, with camera still rolling, the AC brings in the slate **upside down**, calls *"Tail slate,"* announces scene/take, and claps — the log warns that the material was tail-slated.

## The crew's attention during a take

Each role monitors its own concern — the on-set analogue of the engine collecting each role's contribution:

| Role | Watches / listens for |
|---|---|
| **Camera operator** | Focus, composition, framing, movement; whether the mic edges into shot. |
| **DP** | Lighting on actors as they move; camerawork on the field monitor; exposure on the waveform. |
| **Camera assistants** | Focus (pulling when needed) and camera function. |
| **Script supervisor** | How each movement is done; departures from scripted dialogue — all noted per take. |
| **Director** | Every aspect for **emotional truth and intensity** — is the scene focused, does it deliver, what made this take special? |
| **Sound recordist** | Dialogue quality, background, off-noises, whether lines are on-axis and clear of footsteps/set noise. |
| **ADs** | That actors hit marks; that the set stays quiet. |
| **Makeup / hair** | Whether their work wilts under the lights. |
| **Electricians** | That all lights stay on and nothing burns. |
| **Grips** | That rigging holds, lights stay up, scenery is anchored, and the camera hits its marks on tracking shots. |

The scene runs until the director calls **"Cut."**

## Who can call "Cut"

Generally **only the director** — you may let a flawed take run because the actors are reaching something interesting, or overlook a glitch you can cut around or fix in post. Others may abort **only by prior arrangement**: the camera operator on a hopeless framing/focus error or an actor lost from frame; a **stunt supervisor** on imminent danger; very occasionally a prestigious actor. You never want the cast deciding which of their work is usable.

## Another take, circle, or keeper

- Need another? *"Cut, let's go for another"* — then a quiet word to the actors on what you want.
- Successful? *"Cut! … Good for camera?"* → check sound → *"Circle that."* A **circled take** contains usable material; a long scene may circle three or four (with notes like "first part of Take 4, last part of 6 or 7"). Tell the script supervisor exactly what's usable — it saves edit time.
- A take good start-to-finish is a **keeper (best) take**; then move to the next setup.

The **"circle/keeper" lingo** descends from celluloid (marking which takes to print). On digital everything is ingested, so usage is shifting toward *"Cut! … we got it. Let's move on."*

## Coverage, changes, and pickups

- **Closer & reverse shots.** Once the master is in the can, move in per the coverage plan — each setup a new slate, each attempt a new take; different lenses/heights alter space. Backgrounds and lighting may be **cheated** (adjusted invisibly) for a better effect, but the **key direction must match** and the shot must intercut with the master.
- **Shot / blocking changes.** New coverage ideas emerge (merge an MLS+MS; add a close-up of a nice master moment) — confer with the script supervisor so nothing is omitted. Beware **crossing the line** (straying over the scene axis): the floorplan/storyboards show who looks which way, and screen directions must hold or the scene won't cut.
- **Retakes & pickups.** A near-perfect take with a bad line/move is retaken **without stopping the camera** (*"Keep rolling … do the storm section again"*). Anything not in the shooting script is a **pickup shot** (a line of birds on a wire), logged with the scene number and **PU** designation.

## Room tone, continuity, and the strike

**Record room tone first.** When a scene's shots are done, before anything is struck or lights killed, the sound recordist calls the whole unit to **freeze for one minute** and records the location's ambient **room tone (presence/ambience track)** — crucial filler for editing dialogue.

**Continuity sheets.** For each setup the script supervisor records production/date, script scene, camera/lens, slate/take, timing, movements/actions, dialogue variations, successes/flaws, plan changes, pages covered (to the ⅛), and any special instruction to the editor.

**Striking the set.** After room tone the AD calls the strike: camera/sound pack methodically; art stows props; makeup cleans actors; wardrobe/hair collect for overnight repair; the **data wrangler backs up the day onto at least two drives** and spot-checks (a whole day's work); the AD hands out next-day call sheets and colored revision pages; the director thanks the actors and unit; AD/PM confirm the location is clean and undamaged; the circus moves on.

**That's a wrap.** On the last day, at the last location, after the final keeper — the **Martini shot** — the director declares principal photography complete: **"That's a Wrap!"** — and the project moves to postproduction.
