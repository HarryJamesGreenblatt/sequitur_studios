# Chapter 32 — Editing Begins: Getting to Know the Footage

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 32.
> **Scope:** the front end of the assemble phase — ingesting, logging, and syncing footage; reviewing dailies; building the marked-up editing script and the dailies book; making and diagnosing the first assembly. The director's-eye complement to *Grammar of the Edit* Ch. 4 (assessing footage).

## Core idea

Editing begins not at the first cut but at the moment the footage arrives, and the director's first job is to **change hats** — to stop being the planner and instigator of the material and start seeing it with the innocence of a first-time audience. The script and its intentions are now **historical relics, an old map to a rebuilt city**; the only possible film lies within the dailies. Before any assembly can happen, the footage must be organized (ingested, logged, synced), reviewed with fresh and disciplined attention, and distilled into two reference documents — the **marked-up editing script** and the **dailies book** — that record the material *as shot* rather than as planned. Only then does the editor build the crude, baggy **first assembly** whose purpose is to let the film reveal its own nature.

## Transferring, logging, and organizing footage

Good organization is the difference between editing and hunting for materials. Sound data management on set delivers the editorial staff one of several backup drives holding picture and sound files organized by shooting day. The staff **ingest** (or **link**) that footage into a **non-linear editing system (NLE)**, then identify each individual shot as a discrete media file — a **clip** — labeling it with its scene and take number. Cameras and recorders carry metadata capacity to tag scene/take, but in practice this is rarely accurate end-to-end and on student shoots rarely done at all: **assume the editorial staff will label clips themselves.**

- **Bins** — media folders in the NLE's **project window**, one per scene, each holding all picture and sound for that scene (the digital heir to the physical film bin).
- **Logging** — the whole process of labeling and organizing shots by bin and scene number so every length of picture and sound is easy to locate. Done daily during production if the editor is engaged early, or all at once after the shoot.
- **Duplicate drives** — organizing footage in advance lets director and editor each hold a copy, exchange the latest **edit decision list (EDL)** remotely, and demonstrate modifications to each other from the shared "bank." It also lets a replacement editor step in if ever needed.

## Syncing dailies

**Dailies** (or **rushes**) are the raw, unedited footage a production generates; the aim is to review them daily — or as soon as possible — to catch problems and omissions and reshoot **before leaving the location**. Most narrative work uses **double-system sound**: camera records picture while a recordist independently captures audio. After ingest and logging, sound takes are aligned to their picture clips — via the clapperboard bar or a smart slate's timecode — into perfectly synchronized **sync clips**. Done meticulously on set, this is routine. Scene bins may also hold **wild tracks** (non-sync recordings of fluffed lines, voice-over, dialogue), location **SFX**, and **room tone** (interior ambience) for the sound editor to fill dialogue-track gaps.

## Dailies: reviewing and evaluating footage

Digital capture has changed the old rite of viewing dailies — takes can be reviewed instantly on HD field monitors, and editors can transfer, log, sync, and even rough out **scene assemblies** on the spot, letting key personnel monitor performance, lighting, camerawork, and coverage adequacy day to day. Economics sometimes delays low-budget cutting until the shoot wraps.

| Review mode | Purpose and discipline |
|---|---|
| **Crew viewing session** | Best available quality (HD monitor or large projection). **Never let your attention leave the screen** to write — scribble large notes or use a voice recorder. Afterward, **listen rather than debate**: crew reactions preview future-audience reactions, but each member is disproportionately attuned to their own discipline. |
| **Editor + director** | Watched together during and after the shoot. The editor commits resources to memory and voices feelings about performances; the director should **listen attentively**, because immersion with an actor can polarize judgment. The editor is your **first objective audience** and can spot missing coverage, transition material, or continuity — cheaper to fix before leaving the location. |
| **Marathon viewing** | Exposes the material's relativity: recurring actor mannerisms that must be cut around, a principal who threatens to unbalance the film, unexpected moods worth noting. **Sit through everything** — outtakes, false starts, aborted takes — because a "magic moment" can be a lifesaver in the edit. |

The **dailies book** is the shot-by-shot record of first impressions — good, bad, large, subtle. **Gut feelings are seldom unrepresentative**: whatever triggered them is embedded in the material for any first-time audience to feel, and these notes are a lifeline back once memory glazes over. The same essentials can live in the NLE's **clip notes** function, reviewable every time you scan clips. Finally, view all material one scene at a time (well-logged dailies call up in scene order), stopping for discussion; the editor adds the director's choices and cutting notes to the dailies book.

## The editing script

Once logging and viewing are done, the editor or assistant prepares the **marked-up editing script**. For each scene, view every setup a take at a time, then **bracket** what each angle covers, leaving space to write the scene number, an abbreviated shot description, and the take count. **Circle the director's preferred takes** ("keepers") by reference to the dailies book, cross-checking camera, sound, and continuity records. Note the reason for botched takes ("Fluffed line," "Cam framing NG"). A **change of lens counts as a new setup**, since framing and composition differ even if the camera did not move.

Though it may look like a duplicate of the bracketed shooting script (Ch. 24), the editing script records the footage **as shot** — changed locations, altered line readings, simplified setups, new dialogue, actions, or entire scenes not in the original, plus technical departures logged by camera and sound and the continuity notes of the script supervisor. Merged together, it is a **digest of essentials**: what angles cover every moment, which takes hold the preferred performance, and which hold workable alternatives. For more detail the editor digs into the cutting-room copies of the camera, sound, and script-supervisor logs, and the bible of first impressions — the dailies book.

## The only film is in the dailies · Partnership

Now you **change hats**. Empty yourself of prior knowledge and intention; don't be surprised if the editor discerns alternatives faster than you. With shooting over, the editing staff begins a long full-time stretch with a director who often arrives **fatigued, anxious, and morbidly aware of unrealized intentions** — a kind of postpartum state. Relationships vary with temperament:

- **The wise director** discusses the intentions behind each scene, gives special directions, then **leaves the editor in peace** and returns days or weeks later with a fresh, constructive eye.
- **The obsessive director** hovers in the cutting room, watching every action — bearable only if the editor enjoys debating through the process; most prefer to solve the initial problems in bouts of concentration.

Eventually every shot and cut is scrutinized, debated, weighed, and balanced. The editor sits at the center, exerting delicate, sustained leverage against the fixations that close like a trap around virtually every director's heart. (Rosenblum & Karen's *When the Shooting Stops … the Cutting Begins* documents how crazed this mutually dependent marriage can become.)

## Making the first assembly

The **first assembly edit** is a long, very loose version bringing every scene and sequence together in broadest outline. To make one:

- Review the **dailies book** for each shot's impressions.
- Use the **marked-up script** to decide initial coverage — mostly **master shots**, no close-ups or cross-cutting. Assemble the most basic version faithful to the story arc and true to the footage.
- **Don't finesse anything.** No transitions (dissolves, fades), no cutting on action, no overlap/lap cutting, no curing questionable pacing, no worrying about length, balance, or polish — most of it will change.
- **Include two versions** when both seem equally viable or equally questionable; choose later in fuller context.

It is a messy first draft, but exciting: the film starts to reveal its nature. The urge to polish favorite sequences must be resisted — detail work distracts from engaging the film's inherent identity.

## Viewing the assembly · Diagnostic questioning

Run the long, loose assembly **without interruption and without notes** — ingest the whole baggy film as a super-attentive audience would. Fundamental issues come out of the closet: the film is slow, some scenes over-explain or run past a good ending, there are two endings (one false, one intended), a minor character proves stronger than a major one, a hard-won sequence stalls the story. **Kill your darlings — but keep them in a dead-darlings bin** for possible resurrection.

Run it again to test whether first impressions hold, staying in the film's **present** to preserve first-audience innocence. Then list your aims for each sequence, strictly by priority, and interrogate:

- Does the film feel **dramatically balanced**, or does a mid-film peak make the rest anticlimactic?
- Does it **circle aimlessly** before it moves? Where do you feel momentum, and where not?
- Which parts **work**, which **drag** — and why (a scene misplaced, or repeating a prior scene's contours)?
- Which characters held your attention most, which least?
- Was there a **satisfying alternation** of material, or was similar material clumped indigestibly?
- Were there effective **contrasts and juxtapositions** — and more to be made?
- Did a sequence fail because the ground wasn't prepared, or for lack of mood contrast? **Variety matters as much in storytelling as in dining.**
- What **metaphorical allusions** did the material make, and could it make them more strongly?

Afterward, jot the best scenes from memory (most striking first), then glance at the script for what you forgot — **memory discards what fails to contribute.** Deal only with dominant reactions for now.

### Table 32.1 — Assembly problems and possible solutions

| Problem | Possible solutions |
|---|---|
| A scene's **writing** is poor beside the rest | Cut the scene? Shorten? Rewrite and reshoot? |
| **Acting** at fault — predictable rhythms, out of character/focus | Editing helps, not cures; reaction times are often wrong and carry the wrong (or no) subtext — rebalancing can help. |
| Scene **outcome is predictable** | Faulty scene structure? Too long/slow? Wrong place? |
| Two+ sequences make a **similar point** | Repetition advances nothing without escalating pressure — choose and ditch the redundant. |
| **Minor character** contribution redundant | Minimize their presence or cut them entirely. |
| **Subplot** takes too much time / too expository | Minimize or cut; the more dynamic the main characters, the more a viewer tolerates minor gaps. |
| **Dramatic intensity plummets** | Think of a rising/falling emotional temperature: lowering it before the intended peak impairs response. Transposing one or two sequences can work wonders. |

## Resolutions, length, and structure

Now make **far-reaching resolutions** about the film's development — performances, pacing, parallel storytelling, structure, meaning. **Prioritize ruthlessly and correct only major problems after each running.** If structure is awry, reorder scenes and run again with no scene-level refinement; if two principals are imbalanced, bring forward the deficient one. Non-linear editing **preserves every sequence and version** — so **save, label, and date each cut** (assembly v.1, v.2, v.3) to avoid working on a stale version.

The hardest achievement in any art form is to **say a lot through a little.** Stories have a natural span; beginners' films almost always run long and slow, and screen time is not commensurate with artistic stature — recognizing your 30-minute assembly wants to be 15 minutes lets you get radical. The assembly stage is also the moment for **larger structural experiments**: reorder scenes, withhold revelations, intercut, shift POV. Prof. Andrew Lund has students make **"totally radical" cuts** — reverse chronology, no dialogue at all, a two-minute version — not as finals but to break calcified expectations and reveal the material's flexibility; discoveries inevitably feed the final edit. **A good screenplay never guarantees a great film**, because cast, production, and editing introduce new shading — and the director, most encumbered by the film's history, must let it go.

## Leave the editor to edit

Once the next round of changes is agreed, **leave the cutting room until summoned.** Everyone loses objectivity with long exposure to footage; a director returning with a fresh eye better sees where changes work or are still needed. The whole postproduction crew must purge prior expectation to see the new version as a first-time audience will — a hand-to-hand struggle with the medium that takes **Zen mastery to see "what is," not "what was" or "what should be."**
