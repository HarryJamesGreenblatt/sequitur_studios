# Chapter 26 — Developing a Production Crew

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 26.
> **Scope:** how a film crew is assembled, vetted, and organized — **departments, chain of command, who answers to whom, temperament, set etiquette, and production safety.** This is the real-world source for the studio's **Role / Department / Phase** model: it is META to the crew engine itself.

## Core idea

The chapter is titled **developing** — not choosing — a crew, because even experienced technicians must have their work known and be trial-shot before production. On a small unit, crew **temperament directly affects the actors**, so you cast the crew for personal maturity as much as technical skill. Filmmaking is a **village**: check a candidate's reputation through the people they worked for; good work gets recommended and problems surface either bluntly or in a diplomatic silence.

The deeper structure the chapter encodes is an **interdependent hierarchy of departments** with explicit **lines of responsibility**. Every role *answers to* someone; queries flow up through the department head to the DP, who leads the crew while the director's whole energy stays with the cast. That "answers-to" org chart — grouped by department, staffed per phase — is exactly what the studio's crew engine reifies (storyline [0008](../../../context/storyline/0008-the-crew-engine.md), [0012](../../../context/storyline/0012-the-crew-unflattened.md)).

## Developing your own crew

| Dimension | What to develop / demand |
|---|---|
| **Communication** | Shoot rehearsals documentary-style so the set becomes a lab: you evolve a **terse, unambiguous common language** (one operator's "close-up" is another's "medium"), learn each other's expectations, and let the crew build an organic understanding of the cast and your intentions. Expect standards to keep evolving all through production. |
| **Commitment, ideas, values** | Everyone must accept your commitment to the project and to drama's significance, and ideally share your values. Probe favorite films, books, plays, hobbies, travel — this matters most in a **DP** and **production designer**. Tech gaps can be fixed; **immaturity and negativity cannot.** |
| **Team qualities** | A low-budget crew is ~six to ten; one misfit is a black hole that swallows morale. Want: **warmth, a nurturing temperament, sociability, humor, low-key can-do realism, and sustained concentration** over long hours. Filmmaking is all about details. |

**Avoid anyone who:** has a single (usually slow) speed and falls apart under pressure; has a short attention span; forgets or modifies verbal commitments; talks too much; overestimates their abilities; misses deadlines; or thinks they're doing you a favor and treats the job as a stepping-stone (usually bailing mid-shoot).

## The crew's attitude and actors

A well-functioning crew under the **DP's benign but watchful leadership** hugely boosts actors' morale — actors (some on camera for the first time) are **hypersensitive** and register any team member's detachment or disapproval. Rules to brief the crew on:

- Actors may privately seek a crew member's opinion. React with **extreme diplomacy** — be generally supportive (mainly what actors seek). If an actor solicits agreement for a negative attitude or wants something conveyed to the director, stay neutral, then **report discreetly up the chain of command.**
- Never voice criticism that **weakens anyone else's authority**; keep all disagreements out of public view. Actors find visible dissent among those they depend on deeply disturbing.

## Areas of responsibility

**Lines of responsibility.** No team functions unless each role and chain of command is defined. When the director is with the cast, the **DP leads the crew** and decides. In most cases a query goes **crew member → department head → DP**, not to the director. Line producer, AD(s), UPM, and DP exist to take unnecessary burdens off the director.

**Start formal.** Hold to the agreed structure of relationships; everyone concentrates on their own responsibilities and refrains from commenting on others'. As trust is earned, that protective formality can relax by mutual consent. Over time crews also grow **informal roles** — prophet, diplomat, navigator, therapist, crisis manager, and the all-important **jester**. Working well as a group is exhilarating: a team of determined friends is unstoppable.

## Role descriptions by department

Organized under a large feature's customary department structure; this is the **modest core** for a low-budget shoot. The **"answers to"** column *is* the chain of command — the org chart the crew engine encodes.

### Direction department

| Role | Answers to | Responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | the producer | The **unifying vision** guiding most preproduction and production decisions (full role in Ch. 1). |
| **Script Supervisor** | the director | Knowing the planned coverage for every scene, ensuring it gets shot, and monitoring exact words, actions, props, and costumes shot-to-shot. Suggests economies when time/resources run low. Needs fierce concentration, formidable observation/memory, deep editing knowledge, and fast accurate reporting an editor can trust. On student films the **editor** often doubles here. |
| **Assistant Director (AD)** | the director *or* UPM (per function) | See Production department. |

### Production department

In the US the producer's role is primarily fiscal/logistical, and heads this department.

| Role | Answers to | Responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer** | executive producer / investors / studio | Assembling and administering funds, overseeing the whole project, and arbitrating artistic disputes (e.g. star vs. director). Best producers are cultivated, sensitive enablers who **unobtrusively nourish good work**; the danger is the dilettante who, controlling money, distrusts their experts and runs the organic process like a construction job. Always has a checkable track record. |
| **Unit Production Manager (UPM)** | the producer | Day-to-day logistics and money disbursement — the producer's delegate. Issues contracts; books rented gear to camera/sound specs; locates lodging, food, facilities; builds the shooting schedule (with AD and director); arranges dailies; monitors cash flow and pays bills; plans weather contingencies; liaises with the outside world. Needs organization, negotiation, diplomacy, multitasking, and quick accurate decisions about time, effort, money. |
| **Line Producer** | the producer | Preparing the budget (chiefly below-the-line) and adjusting it against actual spend; cash-flow reports; vendor negotiation. Low-budget films often **merge UPM + Line Producer.** |
| **Assistant Director (AD)** | UPM or director (per function) | All legwork and logistical planning: scheduling, locations/permits, getting the right people to the right place, coordinating props/wardrobe/hair/makeup, calling and managing talent, directing extras, barking orders for the director, and often **health & safety**. Needs a good business mind, encyclopedic knowledge of union rules, firm diplomacy, and a voice that wakes the dead. Rarely becomes a director (skills are organizational). |
| **Craft Services** | production | Servicing departments, protecting the set, keeping refreshments (and high-octane coffee) ready. Catering provides a full meal ~every six hours; skimping on food is a serious mistake. Meticulous about dietary needs, allergies, and religious restrictions. |
| **Production Assistant (PA)** | whoever employs them | Anything delegated. Needs a sunny can-do temperament, St. Bernard loyalty, sealed-lips discretion, and juggling ability. On a low-budget shoot the PA carries proportionally more responsibility. |

### Camera department

Camera crew need a visual sensibility, a photography/fine-art background, knowledge of gear theory and practice, decisiveness, and often physical robustness (handholding a 15-lb camera for hours; loading boxes). Best camera people are equable, resourceful perfectionists who can **improvise a simpler solution when time and money run short** — and who don't isolate themselves in tech and dismiss the director's quest for meaning.

| Role | Answers to | Responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| **Director of Photography (DP) / Cinematographer** | the director | All cinematography, aesthetic and practical — **the look of the film**, and the most important crew member after the director. Specifies lighting/camera gear, lenses, formats, color profiles; plans all setups; **leads the crew** while the director attends actors; scouts locations with the gaffer; decides and supervises lighting placement; supervises camera and lighting crews. **Runs tests early** to forestall Murphy's Law. |
| **Digital Imaging Technician (DIT)** | the DP | A smooth on-set digital workflow: image quality, file management/backup protocol, and applying **monitor LUTs** for RAW/Log viewing. On elaborate jobs, on-set color grading (DaVinci Resolve) and custom LUTs that follow footage into post — a DIT can become a major creative collaborator. On low-budget/Rec. 709 shoots the role streamlines to file management, backup, and QC and is called the **data wrangler**. |
| **Camera Operator** | the DP | Handling the camera: positioning (with the director), framing, and all movement (pan/tilt/track/crane/dolly). Reads behavioral cues, makes dramatically logical grab-shooting decisions, and **alerts the director** to wayward focus, bobbled moves, boom shadows, or awkward compositions — because the operator sees the **framed cinematic image**, not the 3D action. |
| **Assistant Camera (AC)** | the camera operator | Everything about the camera. **1st AC** keeps camera/optics prime, enters settings (format, ISO, frame rate, profile, white balance). **2nd AC** runs the slate, sets marks, pulls focus when needed, keeps camera logs, and coordinates full media cards with the data wrangler. |
| **Gaffer** (head electrician; with **Best Boy**) | the DP | Rigging lights and knowing every way to fix, mount, move, or lower anything (including the camera). Grasps the intent behind the DP's lighting; understands instruments and lighting principles. |
| **Grips** / **Key Grip** (+ **Best Boy Grip**) | the gaffer / DP | Strong, practical, organized. The **key grip** rigs lighting per the gaffer and does the skilled job of **moving the camera support** (dolly, crane, truck) mark to mark on mobile shots. On a minimal crew, grips double up on sound/camera assist. Their work is thankless (they set up while production waits, then strike and haul when everyone else is done) — and they're often miraculously resourceful. |

### Sound department

Every sound problem has multiple solutions; the art is **selecting and placing mics, and hearing the difference.** Recordists listen not to meaning but to **sound quality** — buzz, rumble, edginess the uninitiated screen out; musical training instills this ear.

| Role | Answers to | Responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| **Sound Recordist** (a.k.a. production sound mixer) | the DP | Capturing clear, clean, consistent sound: setting up gear, monitoring levels/quality, solving problems. In interiors, lighting and camera take precedence, so mics must be placed **unseen and shadowless** yet capture pristine quality. |
| **Boom Operator** | the recordist | Keeping the mic as close to sources as possible without entering frame or casting shadows — following the script to move the mic to each new speaker; agile, quiet footwork on mobile shots. |
| **Cable Wrangler** (added on complex sets) | the recordist | Managing cables during takes, or serving as second boom. |

Sound work demands patience, a musician's discrimination, and the maturity to be **low on the totem pole**: the recordist defers to shooting, is kept idle then suddenly asked to "fix the mike." The best watch the situation unfold and adapt quickly.

### Art department

| Role | Answers to | Responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| **Production Designer / Art Director** | the director | Designing the film's whole environment to interpret the script — props, costumes, sets, locations, period research. On low budgets the designer sketches and dresses sets personally; on larger ones an **art director** supervises draftspeople, set dressers, and **construction specialists** (carpenters, plasterers, painters, electricians, riggers). Needs fine-arts/design/architecture background, fluent sketching, a color and social-history eye, and project-management skill. |
| **Special Effects Personnel** | the art director | Explosions, fires, collapsing bridges, breakaway windows, models, process shots, CGI, robotics — and anything involving **danger and stunt people**. Point of honor: if the script says it, they can make it. |
| **Wardrobe & Props** | the production designer | Locating, storing, maintaining costumes and properties; master lists so the right item appears on time and in working order. When no wardrobe person exists, actors bring their own — and the **AD double-checks** each actor's next-scene clothes. |
| **Makeup & Hairdressing** | the production designer | Appropriate face/hair (with period accuracy), and quietly **catering to actors' insecurities** so they believe in how they look. Arrives ahead of everyone; after the early rush, watches for repairs. Requires diplomacy and endurance. |

## Set etiquette

A director **sets the tone** of the set. Respect has three dimensions: for the **project**, for the **people**, and for **ourselves** through good work.

- **Do your own job fully.** One person slacking throws everyone off. Lulls aren't invitations to wander for snacks or personal calls — the set can come alive at any moment.
- **Be early.** The industry prizes promptness; unreliability (even at film-school level) ends invitations.
- **Keep a can-do attitude**; stow the smartphone (the reigning distraction) unless using a work app.
- **Respect the team structure** — the chain of command, division of labor, and everyone's area. Give praise where due, never claim others' credit, never humiliate mistakes. Harassment and bullying are addressed at every level; know your program's reporting protocols. Keep **personal animosity** out of it.
- **Respect the public's space** (parks, sidewalks, shops): low profile, no excessive noise, no obstruction, no litter.
- **Respect the location:** leave it in the same or better condition; replace everything exactly as found.
- **Food and breaks:** twelve-hour days are common; a **food break stops all work** (not sandwiches on the run). Keep water on hand and bathrooms near the set.

## Production safety and security

Nothing else matters if a catastrophe occurs. The **commandments**: every filmmaker has a moral and legal duty to keep cast, crew, and public safe (lack of funds is never an excuse); **safety is everyone's responsibility** — if you see something, say something; learn and follow every applicable regulation (union, government, school, location); stay vigilant because anyone can exercise poor judgment.

**Prepare for safety (in preproduction):** assign a **safety coordinator** (UPM/AD on small shoots, a dedicated role on large); scout for hazards (traffic, electrical capacity, structure, hazardous materials, fire, weather, high-voltage lines) and note exits, extinguishers, access; hold **safety meetings** with department heads; put **emergency contact info on every call sheet** (police, fire, ambulance, nearest hospital); hire specialists for fire, autos, stunts, prop weapons, or water; schedule reasonable hours with rest between days; ensure everyone is **trained on their gear** (a YouTube video is not training) and insure the production.

**Maintain common sense** — most accidents come from forgetting it. No shortcuts (don't send an operator up a steep roof); ban distractions; **no alcohol or drugs** (including drowsiness-causing medications); keep the set neat, tape down cables, and **never block emergency exits**.

**Special circumstances:**

| Hazard | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Weather** | Dress for it; provide a heated shelter in cold and shade/water/sunblock in heat; **never shoot in hostile weather** just to stay on schedule — reschedule. |
| **Risky locations** | Verify safety during scouting; abandon structurally unsound sites, hazardous materials, or non-code wiring. Avoid cliffs, soft riverbanks, busy highways. |
| **Picture vehicles** | Extreme caution — distracted (acting) drivers cause accidents. Car mounts rigged/tested only by specialists, never on public roads without a permit. Chases and fast driving require trained precision drivers and prepared vehicles — out of reach for students. |
| **Weapons** | **Never a real weapon.** Prop guns come in rubber, non-firing "function," and blank-firing (which can injure or kill). All require permits and insurance. Rent from a certified house; only trained handlers touch them; treat every gun as loaded; store securely; **notify local police** and arrange supervision — any bystander who sees a gun will call them. |
| **Fire / open flame** | Keep an extinguisher on set; designate a **flame watcher** for even birthday candles; a real fire needs a bonded, insured pyrotechnics expert (many locations forbid open flame). |
| **Stunts** | Simple falls: cast a fit performer who knows how to fall, pad and mat off-screen, rehearse. Anything beyond a standing fall requires **professional stunt doubles and coordinators** — a regular actor can't do it safely or convincingly. |
| **Water** | Electricity + water = electrocution. Prefer available light; never place lights where they can fall in; bounce light from outside the bathroom; station a grip at each unit. |
| **Electricity** | Movie lights harness thousands of watts. Beyond a portable kit, hire a **professional gaffer** (a certified electrician). |

**Keep the set secure:** set up a guarded staging area for gear and personal belongings; never leave anything in cars; losses spike on messy sets and rushed strikes — one person watches the vehicle while others load. Consult the **CSATF** *General Code of Safe Practice* and *Safety Bulletins* for specifics.

## Studio application

- **This chapter is the source blueprint for the crew engine's structure.** The book's department roster maps almost one-to-one onto [`Department`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) (`CAMERA · ELECTRIC · GRIP · EDITORIAL · SOUND · DIRECTION`); the **"answers to"** chain of command is what the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) reconciler and the *DP-leads-the-crew* pattern encode; and "which crew is on call" is [`Phase`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) (`PLAN/SHOOT/ASSEMBLE/SHIP`). Each book role is a studio [`Role`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) that owns a slice of grammar and returns a `Contribution` — see storylines [0008](../../../context/storyline/0008-the-crew-engine.md) and [0012](../../../context/storyline/0012-the-crew-unflattened.md), and [Grammar of the Shot Appendix D](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/appendix-d-crew-positions.md).
- **DP as crew lead → Director as reconciler.** The rule "queries flow up to the DP, not the director" is exactly the studio's split: crew roles ([Cinematographer](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py), [Gaffer](../../../sequitur/crew/lighting.py), [KeyGrip](../../../sequitur/crew/grip.py), [Editor](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py)) each decide their own concern; the `Director` reconciles their contributions into a `Shot` ([0014](../../../context/storyline/0014-the-crew-behaviour.md)).
- **"Cast for temperament, swap the judgment."** The book's warning that immaturity can't be fixed while tech can — and that a role's *attitude* matters — grounds the swappable [`Judgment`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py) seam: a role's reasoning (Heuristic **A** → Persona **B** → Human) is the dial that gives each seat its "temperament."
- **Producer = the human (HITL).** The book's producer (fiscal/logistical, arbiter of disputes, enabler who shields the director) is the studio's human-in-the-loop supplying the [`Brief`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) and holding final say — distinct from the *agent* Director. See the production-instance model in [0005](../../../context/storyline/0005-productions-as-instances-and-output-storage.md) and [architecture.md](../../../context/architecture.md).

*The credits list an army; the engine keeps the org chart — departments, phases, and one clear line of command each.*
