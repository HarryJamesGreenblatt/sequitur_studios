# Chapter 37 — Getting It Out There

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 37.
> **Scope:** The final arc — mastering and archive, deriving distribution copies, building promotional material, and the routes to an audience (video hosts, crowdsourced theatrical, festivals, self-distribution).

## Core idea

A finished film is not a shipped film. Once picture is locked, sound mixed, color graded, and titled, the work must be **mastered** — output at the highest quality to redundant, robust media and archived — and only then derived downward into the many formats each outlet demands. Distribution is a moving target ("like the Wild West"), so the emerging filmmaker's realistic path is **self-distribution first**: free streaming for exposure, curated hosts for imprimatur, and above all **festivals** — the venue where reputation, press, and the attention of real distributors are actually made. The last act of directing is relentless, person-to-person hustle.

## Mastering

The first step out of the computer is a **program master**: output to several hard drives (a master plus safety backups) at the highest quality — **ProRes 422, DNxHD 422** — *and* a copy on a premium tape format (**HDCAM SR**), since many consider tape the more secure archive. Program masters serve two purposes: they seed **distribution copies** in down-resolution formats, and they **archive** the film.

**Principles:**

- **Do your homework** — review the whole workflow from shooting format to master format, get all experts to agree, and map the immediate distribution strategy (where it will screen or stream, and in what formats).
- **Master to robust media, in multiples** — use archive-quality drives, not off-the-shelf budget ones, and store backups in *different physical locations* against fire, flood, or storm.
- **Never master to DVD or Blu-ray** — those are distribution formats, not mastering formats.
- **High-end / theatrical** — Digital Cinema projection must meet **DCI** (Digital Cinema Initiative) specs, produced through a post facility: first an uncompressed, unencrypted **DCDM** (Digital Cinema Distribution Master), then a **DCP** (Digital Cinema Package) compressing video/audio to required codecs (**JPEG 2000** + **PCM WAV**) and aligning to projection standards. This is expensive — do **not** commission a DCDM/DCP yourself; master normally, make festival copies in whatever format is required, and let a purchasing distributor foot the lab bill.

## Distribution copies

From the program masters come the release derivations — a thicket of formats and codecs across **tape, disk, and web streaming**. Plan copies for every plausible outlet:

- **Screening dubs** — generated from a master source at a professional facility; many festivals and broadcasters accept specific tape formats (**HDCAM, DVCPRO HD**). Check requirements — and **never send your master tape**.
- **Web export** — NLEs offer wide export menus; export several formats to cover the range. Hosts publish their own specs: **H.264** is the standard web codec, while Vimeo also accepts **ProRes 422** for ultra-high resolution at much larger file sizes. Check both the host's specs and your NLE's platform presets.
- **Disk authoring** — pro pressing takes an uncompressed QuickTime (MPEG-4) but usually demands a 250-disk minimum; for shorter runs, output **MPEG-2** and burn your own DVD/Blu-ray in authoring software. DVD is fading but still common as a festival screener.

## Promotional material

Most filmmakers dread self-promotion and do it badly — which is exactly why doing it well pays. Build large, appealing **posters** and a **publicity packet** (well-organized, printed on quality stock if hand-delivered, and available as a high-resolution, easily shared **.pdf**):

| Packet element | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Film information** | A "teaser" hook, a synopsis, a brief production narrative |
| **Personnel** | Cast and principal crew, with prior accomplishments |
| **Images** | Evocative production stills at poster/press/web quality |
| **Awards** | Festival awards and selected screenings |
| **Reviews** | Positive press quotes |
| **Contact** | Phone, email, website, social handles |

**Channels beyond the packet:**

- **Social media** (Facebook, Instagram, Twitter) — broad reach for audience-building and community connection; sustain a regular, considered presence.
- **YouTube / Vimeo channels** — collect all your films in one place so links are easy to post and send.
- **Filmmaker website** — once awards and reviews accumulate, a channel is not enough: build a full site with streaming films, press links, awards, an artist's statement, downloadable packets, and contact info (Engel's lean Work / Press / Info site is a model).
- **Screeners** — polished DVD/Blu-ray packages (printed covers, case, extras) sent to festivals and programmers, often bundled with the publicity packet.

## Distribution routes

Distribution changes weekly; today's logical strategy can be obsolete tomorrow, and even capable directors feel at sea. A deal with a major **VOD** outlet (Netflix, Amazon) generally requires a knowledgeable producer or distributor — so the emerging filmmaker usually **self-distributes first** to establish a name, which demands a clear strategy, extensive promotion, and focused social-media audience-building. Festivals remain essential both to *show* the film and to *network*.

### Video hosting sites

Free streaming and public exposure make hosts critical — a channel lets you share films, rough cuts, and trailers with colleagues, funders, programmers, and the public, and a view threshold (say 10,000+ on YouTube) can start returning revenue. **YouTube and Vimeo are the effective duopoly** (Dailymotion, Metacafe, Veoh trail); for maximum exposure the big two are unavoidable. **Curated hosts** — *Short of the Week* (a rolling online festival for shorts), *Snag Films, Omeleto, studentfilmmaker.com* — select what they publish, so acceptance itself confers an imprimatur.

### Crowdsourced theatrical

Services like **Tugg** and **Gathr** secure a theatre in your chosen city and supply some exhibitor resources, but promotion is your job and the screening stays provisional until a minimum of advance tickets sells. Cross that threshold and it proceeds, earnings split with the service. You repeat the push in every community — but public screenings often yield **local press reviews**, whose quotes then promote further screenings, raise the film's profile, and attract distributors.

### Festivals

Better than endless self-distribution is being **bought or handled by a professional distributor**, and nothing generates that better than festival buzz. Festivals proliferate — some specialized (climate, food, dance, LGBTQ), others traditional (Narrative / Documentary / Experimental). Everyone knows the elite (Sundance, Tribeca, Berlin, Venice), but an emerging filmmaker is often better served by **smaller, local festivals** (Rhode Island, the Hamptons, Boulder, Athens, Austin) and dedicated shorts festivals. Submission is overwhelming and costly, so use aggregators like **withoutabox** and **filmfreeway** to research, budget, and submit. Festivals are the primary venue where reputations form and commercial attention gathers — enter every one you can afford, attend as many as possible, and let unplanned meetings become partnerships. Seeing your work before a paying audience is the ultimate rite of passage — thrilling or chastening, and either way it tells you what to make next.

**Working the room:**

- **Personal contacts** — value moves person-to-person; research who's who, introduce yourself by name and film, hand over a flyer with screening time and place, make eye contact, shake hands, press your card. It works.
- **Filmmaker's comments** — if introducing your film, rehearse something brief and gracious, credit others, and invite feedback; in Q&A, open by asking what you'd like to learn, stay brief and modest, and pitch your next one or two ideas in 30 seconds each.
- **After a screening** — thank people at the door, hand out cards, ask for honest email notes, and linger (sober) to make contacts. Collaborators, backers, and supporters materialize at exactly these social moments.

## Studio application

- **This chapter grounds the Producer's SHIP gate — the human-in-the-loop authority.** In the studio the **Producer is the human**, not an agent: they own greenlight, delivery, and distribution. The Director agent reconciler holds no authority past picture-lock and master; everything in this chapter is an out-of-band human concern.
- **Mastering is the studio's output-storage step.** A **Production is the output-storage instance** ([storyline 0005](../../../context/storyline/0005-productions-as-instances-and-output-storage.md)) that holds the rendered artifacts from [`studio.py`](../../../sequitur/studio.py) (video), [`image.py`](../../../sequitur/image.py) (stills), and [`speech.py`](../../../sequitur/speech.py) (sound). "Program master + safety backups + archive tape" is the durable master store; **distribution copies in down-res formats** are per-channel exports derived from that master, exactly the derive-downward pattern the renderers already support.
- **"Getting it out there" is the studio's own published repo.** The [published](../../../context/storyline/0003-published.md) repository ([0003](../../../context/storyline/0003-published.md)) *is* the studio shipping its artifact to the world — the software analogue of the festival/VOD release, with delivery-profile config (ProRes/DCP/H.264) standing in for the codec thicket described here.
- **The locked cut that gets mastered is the Sequence.** [`edit.py`](../../../sequitur/edit.py) holds the timeline that finishing ([Ch. 36](ch36-the-finishing-touches.md)) validates and the Producer masters; delivery format/aspect concerns connect to [Grammar of the Shot Appendix A, *Aspect Ratios*](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/appendix-a-aspect-ratios.md), and the whole ship arc closes the plan→shoot→finish→ship pipeline framed in [architecture.md](../../../context/architecture.md).
- **Lifecycle close:** the "empty out, then the next project forms" beat is the Production instance completing and a fresh one opening — the studio's own loop, with the Producer greenlighting the next Production.

*The final act of directing is logistics and hustle: master redundantly, derive per outlet, and carry the film to an audience by hand — the studio's Producer gate that turns a render into a release.*
