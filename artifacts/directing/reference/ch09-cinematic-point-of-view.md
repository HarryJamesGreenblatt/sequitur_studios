# Chapter 9 — Cinematic Point of View

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 9.
> **Scope:** how the director sets and controls **narrative point of view** on screen — the omniscient/subjective and limited/open axes, the nested POVs of character, Storyteller, and audience, and the director's move from **Concerned Observer** to **Storyteller**. POV is the pivot from which the whole aesthetic follows.

## Core idea

A director's single most important possession is a **point of view**. It is not inherited wholesale from the script — it is a deliberate **interpretive lens** the director assembles from every subjectivity available in the characters, their world, and the storytelling temperament behind them. Because film is a *dramatic* medium rather than a literary one, it seldom addresses the viewer directly; most directors **efface the author** and make the film a transparent window on events that appear to "tell themselves." POV is therefore communicated *invisibly*, through the accumulated choices of framing, blocking, sound, and cutting — and because it sets the aesthetic agenda for practically everything downstream, it is the pivotal first decision a director makes.

## POV in literature vs. film

Literature names its narrating POV cleanly; film cannot, because its author hides.

| | **Omniscient POV** | **Subjective POV** |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | the Storyteller, standing *outside* the story | a character *within* the story |
| **Reach** | unrestricted in time and place — sees every past, present, and future | limited to what one character sees, hears, understands, or *mis*understands |
| **Voice** | any human outlook (irony, hope, cynicism), or a neutral **self-effacing narrator** who withholds comment | **naive**, **knowing**, **retrospective**, or **epic-hero** variants |

Film differs from both. It is nearer to theatre — a stream of behaviour, speech, and action for the audience to witness and interpret — but where the proscenium keeps the spectator's distance fixed and dialogue supreme, cinema channels every moment through **camera, microphone, and editing**. Authorial POV is *initiated* by the screenwriter but *experienced* through the director's handling of film craft. Rabiger names this collective, conductor-like authorship **the Storyteller**.

## The nested points of view

Film POV is layered like nested dolls, from the most restricted consciousness outward to the most embracing:

- **Character POV** — the main character's view encloses the subsidiary characters', though each still holds a mirror to the others.
- **Storyteller POV** — encloses all the characters' points of view.
- **Audience POV** — encloses the Storyteller's and the characters'.

## The central character and implied POV

Most films expose the audience to the awareness of whoever is central, making that character the **point of entry** for empathy and identification. A central character is *implied* by whoever:

1. has the **most screen time** — the most opportunity for the audience to identify;
2. **drives the dramatic trajectory** by their decisions and actions;
3. has **distinctive qualities** that set them apart (viewers identify with the unique and its promise of a singular journey);
4. **awakens the most sympathy or empathy** — even a repulsive lead needs one trait we can identify with;
5. has the **most at stake** — stands to win, lose, or benefit most, and undergoes the most change.

## Subjective character POV

Beyond merely *implying* a lead, a director can deepen identification by filtering events through that character's perceptions — both immediate emotion and broader psychology. This shapes content *and* form. Reinforcement techniques:

- **Present in every scene** — everything the audience learns is filtered through the lead.
- **POV shots** — link the audience's visual perspective to the character's; reserve this for the central character, never squander it on minor figures.
- **Expressive mise-en-scène, camera, and sound** — compositions, scene design, colour, angle, focus, movement, and sound design that externalise the character's emotional and psychological state.
- **Interior access** — flashback, fantasy, associative memory, or dream plunge us intimately inside a character's subjectivity.
- **Explicit narration** — first-person voice-over, or the more extreme direct address to camera, makes the character the *literal* storyteller (the dead narrator opening *Sunset Boulevard*).
- **Subjective camera** — the rarely used device where every character looks *into the lens* so the audience sees through the protagonist's own eyes.

Implied and subjective approaches routinely overlay: a film may mostly *imply* its lead, then intensify into subjective technique at emotional, pivotal, or climactic beats — as *The Silence of the Lambs* does with expressionist framings and sound to push us into Clarice Starling's subjectivity whenever the pressure peaks.

## Limited vs. open information

This is the load-bearing axis. **Limited information** restricts everything the audience learns to what the protagonist learns — the viewer discovers *concurrently* with the character, producing tight, subjective identification. **Open information** includes scenes the protagonist is absent from, handing the audience knowledge the character lacks.

The bomb example makes it concrete: show a car explosion *only* as the D.A. experiences it and the audience is shocked *with* her (limited); show the bomb being planted *first* and the same explosion becomes minutes of dread and suspense (open) — and later revealing the bomber to be her trusted colleague thickens the plot with dramatic irony.

| | **Limited information** | **Open information** |
|---|---|---|
| **Audience knows** | only what the lead knows | more than the lead |
| **Yields** | identification, immediacy, surprise | suspense, dramatic irony, dread |
| **Exemplar** | *Eighth Grade* — Kayla central to every scene | *The Night Manager* — cutaways to the closing trap |

Neither is "best," and most films **combine** them, mixing close and open moments to steer emotion at chosen beats (*Get Out*).

## Multiple characters, multiple POVs

- **Dual protagonists** — two equally weighted POV characters (*Thelma & Louise*; *Half Nelson*, where teacher and student become near-equals in peril across age, race, and class).
- **Multiple POVs** — migrating character to character yields a fuller, even divergent understanding (*Mudbound*'s four perspectives on Jim-Crow racism; *Atonement*'s critical moments replayed from contrasting subjectivities; Altman's ensembles with no dominant POV).
- **Subsidiary / alternative POVs** — brief switches to a minor character or antagonist, or **parallel action** to a concurrent strand, refresh perception and heighten suspense (*Schindler's List* seen largely through the eyes of those around Schindler). Good fiction builds real lives, feelings, and agendas into its minor characters.

## Concerned Observer into Storyteller

Cinema resembles a guardian angel — the **Concerned Observer**: involved, invisible, weightless, empathic, drawn from periphery to centre in search of larger patterns of meaning, but *unable to intercede or express an opinion*. This is the role every director must actively adopt; yet passive feeling alone will not lend a film a voice. The director must convert Observer into proactive **Storyteller**:

1. **Define your story** — a good story told in an especially cinematic way, in a defined genre, with a clear approach people respond to.
2. **Take control** — direct the process; don't be seduced by the technology, or the crew and cast will take over and the tail will wag the dog.
3. **Impose a Storyteller** — instil a distinctive storytelling "voice." The Storyteller is *a character you alone define and play* — a sparkling stream of consciousness made manifest through the film.
4. **Stay the course** — hold the central premise and theme against the erosion of rewrites, surprises, location changes, and fatigue; use the original spark as the standard against which every new suggestion is tested.

## Audience POV

Viewers bring their own cultural and historical experience, so identical footage lands differently on different audiences (*The Messenger* read one way by civilians, another by combat veterans, another again by the bereaved). The director can **influence but not fully control** reception; survival depends on drawing as many viewers as possible into the perspective and universe of the film's world.

## Studio application

- **POV is the sharpest cross-department constraint in the whole corpus — a hard rule, not a hint.** "Limited → never cut away from the protagonist; omniscient → cross-cut to what the hero can't see" directly gates the DP's coverage (`SubjectView` in [`crew/camera.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py)) and the Editor's cross-cutting (`EditCategory.CONCEPT` / parallel action in [`crew/editorial.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py)). When the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) reconciles crew proposals into a [`Shot`](../../../sequitur/shot.py) ([storyline 0014](../../../context/storyline/0014-the-crew-behaviour.md)), the controlling POV is the top constraint the other departments must satisfy.
- **The objective/subjective axis is a rendering-fidelity switch.** Interior access — flashback, fantasy, dream, the *subjective camera* — licenses hallucinated or impossible imagery the [image](../../../sequitur/image.py) and [video](../../../sequitur/studio.py) backends can act on; an objective POV forbids it. The "subjective camera / direct address to lens" device is exactly `ShootingStyle.SUBJECTIVE` in [`camera.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py) (the subject looks into the lens), and subjective *sound* — first-person voice-over, a deafened-soldier soundscape — is a job for the [speech/TTS layer](../../../sequitur/speech.py).
- **Limited-vs-open is a withholding schedule the edit executes.** The Screenwriter and Director set *how much the audience knows and when*; the Editor times the reveal — the same withhold-for-suspense logic in [Grammar of the Edit Ch. 5](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md).
- **Concerned Observer → Storyteller is the argument for a Director `PersonaJudgment`.** The heuristic reconciler ("A") merely *observes* and reconciles; a persona over this Directing grounding ("B") is what supplies the *voice*. The [`judgment.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py) swap from `HeuristicJudgment` to `PersonaJudgment` is precisely this Observer→Storyteller step — and this chapter is the natural corpus for that Director persona ([storyline 0016](../../../context/storyline/0016-abridging-the-screenwriters-taxonomy.md)).
