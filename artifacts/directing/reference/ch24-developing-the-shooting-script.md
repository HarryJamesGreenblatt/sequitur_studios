# Chapter 24 — Developing the Shooting Script

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 24.
> **Scope:** the **shooting script** — the screenplay marked scene-by-scene with the director's shot strategy — plus the coverage methods, POV logic, floor plans, storyboards, and blocking that produce it. This is the PLAN-phase bridge from script to the shoot crew's shot list.

## Core idea

The **shooting script** is the version of the screenplay you take into production: the director and DP mark it, scene by scene, with the specific **shots, angles, and camera moves** planned for each. There are no right answers to shot strategy — type and quantity depend on the scene's dramatic weight and shape, your visual conception, and the time/money available. The governing constraint: **what you gather in the field determines the success of your editing.** If you recorded rehearsals documentary-style, review them to learn which positions, blocking, and moves already work, and derive your coverage from that.

The shooting script is the **creative and technical blueprint for the entire shoot**: distributed to the core team (DP, art director, sound mixer) so they can spec resources, and to the producing team (producer, PM) so they can schedule.

## The general aesthetic

Before covering any single scene, imagine the film's **overall shooting/editing aesthetic** — not to shoot every scene identically, but to establish broad visual coherence. *The Hurt Locker* uses **verité**: handheld, long lenses, shaky movement, rapid jump cuts. *Ida* uses a **mise-en-scène** approach: rock-steady, carefully composed and lit, long takes (one or two shots per scene). Both are perfectly matched to their material. Methods can be mixed and matched per scene's need.

## Coverage methods

| Method | How it works | Buys you | Costs you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Master scene (coverage)** | Shoot the scene from multiple angles: **master** (wide, all characters/action) → **reverses** (singles of each speaker/reactor) → inserts, cutaways, POV. Actors repeat action so overlaps give the editor clean cut points. | Enormous editorial flexibility over rhythm, arc, emphasis; remarkably **invisible** style. Part of the continuity system; used in the vast majority of films. | Time, continuity coordination, repeated takes. |
| **Long-take** | Actions play out over extended periods, sometimes one shot per scene; usually needs a mobile camera and intricate camera+actor blocking. | Immediacy and emotional momentum uninterrupted by cuts; some actors thrive on the unbroken continuum. | You **can't cut around** acting/camera errors (one flub trashes the take); the editor can't rebalance rhythm/pace afterward. |
| **Short-take** | Many angles cut fast; oppositional movement within/between frames; energy over logic/continuity. | Euphoric dynamism and tension in critical moments. | Exhausting over long stretches; demands heavy audience effort; material feels overtly manipulated. Use judiciously. |

## Fixed versus mobile camera

- **Tripod (fixed):** steady, controlled frames from LS to ECU without crowding the actor; lets the viewer absorb mise-en-scène detail. But moves are limited to pans/tilts — no repositioning, no immersion.
- **Dolly/crane:** smooth movement through a preplanned cycle; brings audiences through the film's spaces. But demands precision (cast hit "marks"), sacrifices spontaneity, and greatly extends setup time.
- **Handheld:** best when you need to shoot fast and capture semi-improvised performance. In skilled hands (Ackroyd, Alberti, Lubezki, Morrison on *Fruitvale Station* — fluid and affectionate, restless when emotions spike) it's a dynamic statement; in inexperienced hands, hours lost to home-movie footage.

## Subjective versus objective vantage

The two camera presences imply different **observing consciousnesses**: the mobile/reactive camera feels **subjective** — involved, susceptible to the action; the composed/controlled camera suggests settled **objectivity** in which every frame detail vibrates with meaning. Camerawork alone can shift a film's voice. Holding either mode exclusively grows predictable; shifting between them *with justification* is potent.

## Relatedness: separating or integrating by shot

Composition alters what a scene *feels* like. Two people isolated in intercut singles feels different from intercut **over-the-shoulders** (visibly connected in time and space), and both differ from an unbroken **two-shot** (physical relationship most pronounced). Which pattern to use depends on the relationship you mean to build between characters and viewer — and it is chosen deliberately, before the camera rolls. The same logic governs **foreground/background** juxtaposition (a hungry character framed against a huge hamburger poster) and **depth of field** (shallow DoF blurs the crowd to isolate a lonely character). These are lens-choice and camera-position decisions.

## The camera as an observing consciousness

Often the camera reveals not just the subject but its **context and an attitude toward both** — looking down, up, or peering between tree trunks each implies a different way of experiencing the action. Make the location a meaningful environment that *responds* to participants' actions and sightlines, so the audience shares the consciousness of someone intelligent picking up clues to hidden tensions. Routine coverage that merely turns toward whatever moves (as a dog does) is lifeless; regard the camera as an **astute observer thinking ahead**. A turbulent flea market wants a wandering handheld buyer; a church service wants rock-steady placement, because that's how anyone present experiences it. Always ask: *where does the majority of the telling action lie?*

## Point of view

POV is not merely "what so-and-so sees" in the moment — it's the **sum of impressions conveying how a character undergoes events**. The top priority for each scene: decide **whose POV it favors**, and composition, placement, and editing flow from that. Answers come from script logic and instinct; if there's no overriding determinant, the editor decides later — provided you shot enough coverage. A child witnessing his parents' argument can be shot to be *the boy's* scene, *the father's*, or *the mother's* depending on subtext ("child realizing he's a pawn" vs. "parents too bitter to care"). **Making no choice produces faceless, soulless filmmaking.** POV also **migrates** within a scene; and the more detached **Omniscient Storyteller** vantage is a valid relief valve (Shakespeare's comic interludes) — sustained, unvarying pressure is self-defeating.

## The heart of directing: the Storyteller's point of view

Do you want to **document** happenings like a news service (surveillance), or **tell** them with flair (inflection)? The Storyteller has an active, critical eye for contradiction and irony, raising questions, implying a mind and heart at work. Give your Storyteller an **identity** and an **attitude** — the psychic lens through which the audience experiences the story — and infuse it into every aspect of the film (puzzlement, delight, censure, regret, fascination). Do this and the film acquires the humor and intelligence of work imbued with human character. These questions must be answered *before* shooting, not discovered in the edit.

## Creating the shooting script: shot selection scene by scene

Coverage reflects a scene's function, your instincts, the actors' energy, and hard limits (locations, gear, budget, crew). Some scenes need more shots; some need fewer; one perfectly placed move can beat relentless mobility; a juxtaposition can work better in a single frame than in two cuts. **Elevate critical scenes with sufficient coverage; don't over-determine small moments.** By the end you should have determined *all* the shots covering *every* scene. Diagnostic questions, by category:

| Category | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Dramatic context** | Scene's function? Ruling mood/tone? Who learns/develops (→ POV)? Where's the turning point? (Plan the optimal shot for it, then build coverage toward and away.) |
| **Environment** | What must the scene show to establish it? Orientation early or delayed? Relationship of subjects to environment — shown in one shot or by juxtaposition? |
| **Point of view** | Whose POV at each significant moment? Whose global POV predominates (subjective/objective)? When/why does POV change? How is the Storyteller's attitude made evident? |
| **Eyelines** | Significant eyelines? Where do they change? How is the camera aligned to eyelines and their changes? |
| **Space & movement** | Can space between characters signal dominance/retreat? Correct visual perspective (lens)? Off-screen space vs. the frame? When/why do characters move — follow them, or hold on another? What **axis** does each follow? |
| **Camera movement** | When, how (type), and why (motivation) should it move? What feelings does the move create? Does it reveal or conceal? |
| **Composition & isolation** | What to juxtapose within-frame vs. by editing? Who/what to isolate (the lone misfit vs. the pack; the phone that won't ring)? |
| **Rhythm** | How do shooting/editing rhythms support the scene and performances — and relate to adjacent scenes? |

Be prepared to **cover key moments more than one way**: the mother breaking her wedding glass wants her beat *plus* a spectrum of reactions (son's anger, daughter's surprise, husband's amusement) so you can find the best inflection later. If a line risks being unintelligible, shoot two or three versions — "not compromise but survival." Scan every scene for safety coverage — **insert shots** (magnify detail within a shot: coins in a hand) and **cutaways** (detail outside it: a ticking clock, a startled passerby) — to bridge shots that may not match. ("When in doubt, cut to seagulls.")

## The floor plan and bracketed shooting script

**Floor plans** (**overheads**) are bird's-eye schematics — walls, furniture, character positions/movements (arrows), and planned camera angles — the essential pre-visualizing tool for coverage. Build the shooting script and floor plan **in tandem**:

**Bracketing the script:**
1. **Number every scene** sequentially (a scene = integrity of time and place; new location or day→night = new scene).
2. **Read and imagine** the scene visually (one shot for "man delivers parcel," full continuity coverage for others).
3. **Bracket each intended shot** — a vertical line through all dialogue/action it covers, marking where each shot begins and ends. Allot generous **overlaps** on strong actions (rising, turning to leave) for editorial options.
4. **Letter each shot** (A, B, C…) so every setup has a unique ID (Scene 14 → 14-A…14-F).
5. **Add a shot-size description** (14-A = MS, 14-B = MLS 2-shot, 14-C = MCU; plus OTS and POV).
6. Ensure **every line and action has at least one bracket** through it. Where one shot begins and another ends *is* an edit point — and **the best place to cut is always on a strong physical movement**, so overlap (duplicate) those actions across shots.

**On the floor plan:** draw walls/doors/windows/furniture and the characters' movements/stops (informed by rehearsal blocking); mark each **camera position** ("Angle #1 on Dana"), noting which script shots come from it. Shots from one position (14-A and 14-D) are scheduled **back to back** to conserve camera/lighting resets.

Shooting order follows: **wide/master shots first** (they set performance level, take most lighting, and establish lighting patterns), closer shots after (must match master's acting and lighting logic, with latitude called **cheating**), and reaction shots/inserts/cutaways last — often shot after talent leaves ("off the clock").

## Storyboard, lines of tension, blocking

The **storyboard** is a shot-by-shot rendering from the camera's view, drawn in editing order like a comic strip, in the shooting format's aspect ratio, reflecting shot size, angle, perspective, and movement. Software (Storyboarder, FrameForge) helps; even stick figures suffice to work out composition.

**Lines of tension** are the invisible lines of strong feeling (and often sightlines) between the key people/objects in a scene. They justify camera angles and imply the POVs tied to particular characters. In the parents-argue-child-watches scene, the mother–father axis favors the adults; choosing the boy–parent axes instead favors *his* anguish. Coverage follows: master over the boy's shoulder (audience aligned with him), his looking shot (MCU), his POV shots, plus neutral OTS along the parents' axis to modulate objectivity — tighter singles there would wrongly hand POV to a parent. **The closer the camera is to a line of tension, the more subjectively involved the audience feels.**

**Blocking** (staging) — choreographing character movement through space — is mostly worked out in rehearsal on a location mock-up, then reworked against the real location. Unlike theatre, cinema blocks for a **360° camera**, so movement is always drawn onto the overhead because it dictates camera placement, lighting, and lines of action. Blocking's jobs, escalating in expressiveness:

| Objective | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Scene utility** | Simply stage the written action (man rises to answer a knock). |
| **Describe the space** | Motivate revealing the location (crossing the room shows the filthy apartment). |
| **Reveal internal world** | Movement exposes thought/feeling (he stops short, grabs a jacket, climbs out the window before the door is smashed). |
| **Illustrate relationships** | Movement in relation to another reveals the bond/subtext (he keeps moving away as his wife follows). |
| **Inflect actions** | Staging adds subtext to dialogue (she cleans furiously, gets in his face — "I'm fantastic!"). |

When characters move and **regroup** to face new screen directions, treat those moves as **reset transitions** — each establishes a new axis of action and a new compositional phase, so early and late reaction shots can't be interchanged and the scene can't be freely restructured later. *Movements that lead to regrouping must be shown on-screen.* Choreograph movement for significance; limit it so each move matters.

Finally: **use your intuition** (play each scene out in your head; judge from what you feel, not theory) and **work within your means** — few impressions can't be achieved simply; whole masterpieces (Ozu) were shot with a normal lens on a tripod. Fight for simplicity: *simple is strong, less is more.*

## Studio application

- **The PLAN→SHOOT bridge, in code terms.** The shooting script *is* the shot list the shoot crew executes: each bracketed, lettered setup (14-A = MS) is one `Shot` in [shot.py](../../../sequitur/shot.py), whose fields the Cinematographer/Gaffer/KeyGrip own ([camera.py](../../../sequitur/crew/camera.py), [lighting.py](../../../sequitur/crew/lighting.py), [grip.py](../../../sequitur/crew/grip.py)). The chapter's "general aesthetic" (verité vs. mise-en-scène) is precisely the DP's `ShootingStyle`; fixed/mobile/handheld is the KeyGrip's `CameraMovement`. The scene-by-scene diagnostic table is the interrogation a PLAN-phase Director runs to fill a `Brief` before dispatch in [engine.py](../../../sequitur/crew/engine.py).
- **Bracketing overlaps = the editorial seam.** "Cut on a strong physical movement" and "overlap the action across shots" are the *production-side* guarantees that make the post layer's cut points valid — the same law *Grammar of the Edit* states from the cutting room ([ch05-when-to-cut](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md), [ch01-the-editing-process](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch01-the-editing-process.md)). Coverage is what lets [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py) assemble a `Scene`/`Sequence` from `Clip`s at all; without overlaps, [cutter.py](../../../sequitur/cutter.py) has no match-cut to make. See also *Grammar of the Shot* [ch05-shooting-for-editing](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/ch05-shooting-for-editing.md).
- **POV and lines of tension = a coverage control surface.** "Whose POV does the scene favor?" is the same axis the Screenwriter's Taxonomy models ([ch07-point-of-view](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch07-point-of-view.md)) and which the taxonomy's POV→camera-coverage through-line drives. Storyteller attitude is a **Voice** decision ([ch05-voice](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch05-voice.md)) that should propagate down into angle/size/isolation choices — the reconciliation job of [director.py](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py). *(Overlap flag: POV here (Directing Ch. 24/9) vs. Taxonomy Ch. 7 — reconcile into one POV representation.)*
- **Grounds the Director-as-reconciler and the AD.** Shot selection, floor plan, storyboard, and blocking are the Director's PLAN-phase deliverables; the AD generates the scene numbering/breakdown scaffold (see [ch23](ch23-planning-the-visual-design.md)) and, with the producing team, turns the marked script into schedule — the subject of [ch25](ch25-line-producing-and-logistics.md). The crew roster behind these seats is *Grammar of the Shot* [appendix-d-crew-positions](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/appendix-d-crew-positions.md).
