# Chapter 35 — Working with Music

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 35.
> **Scope:** How music enters a film after picture lock — scratch tracks, the spotting session and cue sheet, licensed vs. composed score, working with a composer, and the discipline of when *not* to score.

## Core idea

Music is the last great transformation of a cut: after months submerged in the material, a well-judged score lifts editor and audience alike into a fresh relationship with the story. Its power is also its danger. The strong choice is almost never to **illustrate** — to duplicate what the picture already says — but to **counterpoint** it, offsetting the visible with an unexpected emotional register. Malick and Zimmer scored the Guadalcanal assault in *The Thin Red Line* with slow, brooding intensity instead of martial triumph, forcing contemplation of war's brutality rather than identification with victory. Because music is infinitely malleable, it earns its place only when a director knows exactly what feeling it must carry — and where silence would carry more.

## Choosing music: scratch and the counterpoint principle

You learn what works only by trying it. **Scratch music** is recorded music laid temporarily against key sequences during the rough cut to test tone, instrumentation, mood, and association — a placeholder that resembles the score you hope to commission, swapped out when the composer delivers. Obvious pairings (surf-rock for California, screechy violins for horror, orchestral swell for romance) read as cliché the moment they are familiar. Playing *against* the obvious yields insight. A radical variant: for *Chernobyl*, Guðnadóttir built the score from field recordings of the plant itself — the intricate buzz of a static door became an instrument — an organic, anti-underscoring approach that refuses to tell the viewer what to feel.

**What a score can do:**

| Function | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Tonal impression** | Color a scene, location, action, or single character |
| **Interiority** | Signal invisible motives; carry integrity or menace a character withholds |
| **Foreshadowing** | Hint at significant action to come |
| **Structural phrasing** | Supply rhythm; bracket transitions between scenes or acts |
| **Stings** | Short melodic fragments that punctuate — best when part of a larger picture |
| **Rescue** | Lend an uninspiring sequence a subtext that accelerates its forward movement |

## Spotting: score vs. source music

**Spotting** is viewing the fine cut to decide where music is needed and where it comes from. Two kinds:

- **Score music** — overlaid, non-diegetic, from outside the film's world; comments on or enhances the action from without (the Zimmer cue above).
- **Source music** — diegetic, emerging from the here-and-now of the scene (the Yardbirds concert in *Blow-Up*, whose driving performance derails the protagonist's search). Source music affects the action even though it is usually cut in during post.

As start and stop points are fixed, the editor logs them on a **music cue sheet** — the same whether the source is licensed, composed, or both.

## Licensed music: limitations and rights

Pre-recorded music serves as score or source, but carries three burdens:

1. **The cut becomes hostage to the track** — the editor feels obliged to cut picture to the recording's rhythm and pacing.
2. **Prior associations** — popular songs drag audience prejudice and memory in with them.
3. **Rights** — clearance is the heaviest cost, often a full-time job even for shorts. (*Blow-Up*'s source song had to be re-lyricked and renamed to clear.)

**Never assume availability.** The worst time to negotiate with a rights-holder's lawyers is when they sense your film now *depends* on their client's recording — your weakest bargaining position.

**Music libraries** sell royalty-free files and mix-and-match loops per download (e.g. neosounds). Serviceable for genre utility — chase, tension, feel-good — but slim for original drama. "Non-commercial" use pays a nominal fee; any distribution or broadcast deal triggers a far larger **mass-use license**.

## Collaborating with a composer

Original music obviates clearance entirely and leaves you free to use it as you wish. Vet a composer by their prior work — **across media, not only film** — to confirm the idiom fits; test existing music against your cut to gauge fit; and talk to their past collaborators about work habits before committing. An experienced composer likes six weeks or more for ~15 minutes of music in a 90-minute feature, though deadline pressure often compresses this to three, ending in all-night composing and score-printing.

**Beginning work.** Composers are hired late and work under pressure — more time yields better music. Ideally the composer reads the screenplay and sees the first complete cut, then probes what the director wants the music to *accomplish* rather than arriving with preconceptions. From the film's characters, settings, and content come basic melodic **themes** and, for particular characters or situations, a **leitmotif** (recurring theme) — best when there is time for era- or culture-specific research.

**The tools.** Composers work in **DAWs** (Digital Audio Workstations) such as Cubase and Logic Pro — many tracks, plug-ins, **MIDI** integrated with live recording, and QuickTime video support so music is built against an accurate cut. **Talk in feelings, not theory:** describe what the audience should feel, or what a character feels but does not show. Bring recorded excerpts for whatever words cannot reach. Composers routinely surface things in your film you did not know were there.

**When scratch persists.** A beloved scratch passage both signals your intent and raises a barrier — the composer must extract what you value and then *outgun* the temp with an original solution. Use scratch to explore during editing, but ask the composer whether to keep it in viewings.

## The cue sheet and composing to sync

Once content is locked, director, editor, and composer screen the film — ideally with burned-in timecode showing cumulative timing — and discuss tone, tempo, function, relationship to picture, and how time passes. Start points hang on **visual cues** (a reaction shot, a car pulling away) or **dialogue cues**. Director and composer agree each segment's start, stop, style, and tone while editor and composer build the **music cue sheet** (timecode in/out plus a cue note per segment — the counterpart of the SFX spotting sheet). Starting a cue is easy; *ending* one without leaving the audience deprived is the craft — commonly you conclude or fade under the entry of something newer or more commanding (a noisy street, the first line of a new scene). The composer leaves with the cue sheet, full notes, and the locked QuickTime; cues get added, dropped, or renegotiated in the writing, because badly placed music is worse than none. A score may travel a narrative arc of its own — Legrand's *The Go-Between* opens on a plangent Mozart-inflected theme and deepens into a full tragic voice as the story's meaning unfolds.

**Composing to sync points.** Write to precise timings, honoring **spot effects** (a tire screech, a dog bark). Instrumentation must not fight dialogue or foregrounded effects — keep the arrangement uncluttered under speech — though a dense diegetic track can sometimes be *replaced* wholesale by music, and musical punctuation can stand in impressionistically for a welter of naturalistic sound. Supply the composer a well-advanced soundtrack, not the bare edit dialogue, especially for cinema exhibition where powerful systems expose everything. Live-recorded scores are marked with cumulative timing so the conductor hits sync points (a sting on the motorcycle at 27s, the revolver at 01:16); MIDI-built scores fit at source against a QuickTime scratch, so sync is not an issue.

## When to score — and when not

Music suggests the hidden: withheld expectation, interior mood, burgeoning feeling (Herrmann's all-violin *Psycho*). It is natural to melodrama and hardest to write tastefully for comedy. Once it sets a mood it should get out of the way and perhaps return later; silence is often the loaded choice, and the rhythms of action, camera, montage, and dialogue are already a kind of music — do not paint the lily. Music may foreshadow and build tension but must never give the story away nor **"picture-point"** by commenting too closely on the visible. Disney's **"Mickey Mousing"** — trills and percussion nailed to every on-screen movement — smothers rather than serves. Wall-to-wall scoring (as in many films of *Suspicion*'s vintage) flattens a film into perpetual melodrama and blocks the audience's own judgment; less is now considered more, and a bare rhythm without melody can be exactly enough. A 40-minute film might carry ~30 cues, from stings to extended passages, with distinct identities for main plot and subplots or characters — kept discrete (especially in key) through crosscutting. Once decided, cues should not change without compelling reason.

## Music editors and fitting

A live session is too expensive to insist on exact fit, so the editor attends and confirms which few-frame picture adjustments are possible. **Music editors** specialize in cutting and fitting music — sourcing period material, protecting a composer's intent through the fluid late edit, or acting as the director's "hatchet man" to force film and music together. Often instrument- or composition-trained, they are indispensable to musicals, much of which is shot to playback on set.
