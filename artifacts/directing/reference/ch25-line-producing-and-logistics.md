# Chapter 25 — Line Producing and Logistics

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 25.
> **Scope:** the practical dimension of production — **scheduling, budgeting, insurance, workflow, shooting formats, and camera/sound choices** — the domain of the producing team (producer · line producer · UPM) that protects the director from logistics.

## Core idea

**Line producing** is the coordination of everything practical: organizing time and personnel; coordinating locations, props, equipment, costumes; dealing with labs and vendors; handling budgets, contracts, payroll, and insurance. It is the domain of the **producing team** — producer, **line producer**, and **unit production manager (UPM)** — and you cannot make a movie without them. Good line producing **undergirds the crew and shields the director** so that everything the creative plan requires is there and ready when production begins. The director meets the producing team regularly to elaborate needs and lock arrangements; at the final preproduction meeting every department head — producer, UPM, director, **AD**, **script (continuity) supervisor**, DP, art director, head of sound — presents their breakdown and last corrections are made.

Read across the studio's own architecture, this chapter grounds the **Producer working the Production board**: scheduling, budget, and workflow are the buckets the human producer manages while the agent crew executes against them.

## Scheduling the shoot

Scheduling is set by the **UPM and AD**, double-checked by the director and principal crew (especially script supervisor and DP); higher budgets add a **line producer** for day-to-day supervision. Software (Movie Magic, Gorilla) distills the script into **stripboards** showing at a glance which scenes/characters shoot at each location. Aim for the **shortest practicable schedule** — working days translate directly to cost — while budgeting educated guesses for contingencies. Factor in:

- Availability of **actors and crew**; availability of **locations**
- Locations and **travel** requirements
- **Costs** at each stage (talent, equipment, crew, facilities)
- Scenes affected by **weather** or special circumstances
- Conditions on rented props/equipment (a luxury car only on weekends; an electrician before a generator)
- **Lighting-setup complexity** and power requirements
- **Time of day**, so available light comes from the right direction

### Locations and shot order

Films are rarely shot in story order — screenplays are **broken down and rearranged for efficiency**. Because lighting eats the most time, **all scenes at one location are shot together** regardless of script order (three INT. CLASSROOM scenes across three acts → light that room once, shoot back to back). Within a scene, order follows coverage logic: **wide/master shots first** (most lighting; they establish lighting logic, spatial relationships, blocking, and performance intensity), **closer shots after** (must match the master), **cutaways and inserts last** (shot after actors leave, or as **pick-ups** by a small **second unit**). The non-sequential shoot can disorient actors, so the **director's prime duty is to orient them** to where their character is in the narrative and what they've just come from — or they'll be in the wrong emotional frame when the camera rolls.

### When to shoot in script order

Sequential shooting benefits films that depend on **graduated character development** (the king's decline in *The Madness of King George*), films built on **improvisation** (Refn: "you paint the movie as you go along"), or **single-interior, small-constant-cast** stories where out-of-order shooting offers little advantage.

### Scheduling key scenes, performances, and demands

- **Key scenes** (on which the whole film depends — the heroine must credibly fall for an unstable man) can't be shot **too early** (cast still green) or **too late** (failure voids weeks of work). Nail it and everything lifts; if it bombs, fix it in rehearsal and reshoot before committing the bulk of the film.
- **Camera nerves** kick in when shooting starts; multiple takes take a toll, and actors instinctively **conserve energy** across takes/angles. Mitigate by predetermining the edit and shooting **less omnivorously** — but as a new director, err on the side of **enough coverage**.
- **Emotional-demand priorities:** delay extreme scenes (nudity, violent anger) until actors are comfortable with roles, each other, and crew; clear non-essential crew; schedule draining scenes **late in the day**.
- **Weather/contingency:** schedule **exteriors early**, and arrange **cover sets** — standby interiors — against uncooperative weather.

### Allocation of shooting time

Crews take **setup time** each morning to light while the director rehearses the coming scene. Plan **4–6 minutes of edited screen time (≈ 4–6 script pages) per 10-hour day** — lower with travel, elaborate setups, difficult moves, or relighting. The continuity supervisor reports screen time shot each day.

### Under- or over-scheduling

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Start light** | The first days are alarmingly slow while a new crew builds communication. |
| **Be realistic** | Most units expect to shoot too much in too little time. |
| **Over > under** | You can shorten an over-long schedule, not lengthen a too-short one — commitments and resources forbid it. |
| **Turnaround** | Standard is **12 hours** between wrap and next call (10 minimum). |
| **Day length** | Target **10 hours** (occasionally 12). Beyond that, diminishing returns: 14-hour days breed sloppy errors, evaporating commitment, collapsing morale — and, worst, **fatigue compromises safety**. |

### The call sheet

The PM and AD translate the schedule into daily **call sheets** distributed to the whole crew, stating: (1) what portion of script shoots that day, (2) who is needed, (3) when each must arrive (allowing crew setup and cast makeup/rehearsal), and (4) how to get there.

## Budgeting the film

The **budget** determines how much money and resources you have — known **before** production, or you risk sticker shock and paralysis. Ideally: start from script → cast/locations/visual-design/needs breakdown → raise funds → distribute across needs with a **price line for every item**. Use **pessimistic figures**. Budgeting is the producing team's domain, and it directly shapes how the director balances interpretive aims (rehearsal, crew size, coverage) against resources. Key questions: what's in the bank; what's realistically still to come; what "in-kind" resources exist; what the schedule will cost; whether funds cover it; where savings or deferrals are possible.

Expenses split at **"the line"** — the division between preproduction and production:

| **Above-the-line** (creative/rights, set before production) | **Below-the-line** (production/post execution) |
|---|---|
| Story rights · screenplay · producer's fee · director's fee · principal actors' fees | Production-unit salaries · cast/stand-ins/extras · art department (sets, props, wardrobe, hair/makeup) · studio & location rentals · camera/electrical/sound/grip/rigging gear · digital media storage + backups · catering/hotel/living · transportation · legal & production insurance · music (rights, composition, SFX) · SFX/VFX/CGI/titles/compositing · postproduction (mix, grade, mastering) · distribution copies & publicity · festival fees |

Every budget carries a **contingency of ~10%** — the Murphy's-Law surcharge for equipment failure, weather, and reshoots.

### Insurances

Not optional at any scale: union actors can't work without proof of insurance, and rental houses and locations demand it. Accidents happen (a $10,000 lens on the sidewalk). Policies may include preproduction indemnity, workers' compensation, producer's indemnity, consequential loss, errors & omissions, employer's/public/third-party liability, third-party property damage, equipment, sets/wardrobe/props, vehicles, and fidelity guarantee — plus special cover when working abroad. Research exactly what your project requires.

> **Software:** professional paperwork uses recognized budget/scheduling packages — **Movie Magic** (industry standard), **Gorilla** (indie), **Chimpanzee** (student). Their relational databases propagate any change across budget, coverage, and schedule instantly, and can generate breakdowns, call sheets, and contact lists while monitoring daily cash flow.

### Caution: equipment overload

Resist padding the kit "just in case," and beware DPs out to pad a reel with nifty toys. Early in a directing career, you and the crew will struggle to master **basic** gear — advanced equipment costs time and can be a **safety** issue (the students who rented a drone and got one inadequate shot in two days because no one could fly it). Insisting on techniques the crew can't execute is rarely successful and often expensive. The **sound department**, however, legitimately needs reserve inventory to adapt quickly to lighting/blocking changes.

## Workflow

**Workflow** is the technical stage-and-format path a project must follow — image and sound formats, editorial format, finishing, mastering, and distribution. Varieties are myriad and each impacts budget, process, and exhibition; losing your way is expensive. Settle formats in preproduction meetings with DP, sound mixer, editor, and post facility — **everyone must be on the same technical page before a frame is shot.** Five questions:

1. **Shooting format?** HD (Rec. 709), Log, or RAW?
2. **Editing?** Format, codecs, frame rate, resolution?
3. **Finishing?** Inside the NLE, or third-party workstations/post-house (grade, mix, VFX, titling)? What file types?
4. **Mastering?** HD files, uncompressed media?
5. **Exhibit/distribute?** Broadcast HD, Blu-ray, web streaming, or Digital Cinema projection?

### Shooting formats and the "look"

The shooting format ripples the whole workflow and governs the color palette available for the final **look** — one of the most important creative choices. Modern grading lets you **design the look in preproduction** (develop and test color settings; monitor footage on set that resembles the final grade) rather than discovering it in post. Flexibility depends on two factors: **bit depth** (color information — the range of colors reproducible) and **dynamic range** (brightness information — the stops of luminance recorded with detail before clipping to pure white or crushing to pure black). More recorded data = more grading latitude. Three options:

| Format | What it is | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard HD (Rec. 709)** / **Cine Gamma** | ATSC HD spec, consistent across shoot/display/broadcast. **Cine Gamma** rolls off highlights/shadows to fake a "film look" (≈ 9–10 stops), baked in. | **WYSIWYG** — usable straight from camera to edit to display. | Limited color space + dynamic range (7–8 stops raw); noisy shadows; **clipping** highlights; look is "baked in." |
| **Log Profile (Log Gamma)** | Logarithmic encoding packs maximum sensor color/luminance; footage looks **flat, dull, grey** — not the final image. | ≈ 12 stops, far more color values; huge grading latitude; look **not baked in** (source stays malleable). Very popular low-budget. | **Requires grading**; needs a **LUT** to view a representative look on set. |
| **RAW** | Records the sensor's whole binary torrent — no frame rate, white balance, color space, or compression imposed. Needs conversion software to view. | Maximum flexibility for grade, exposure, and CGI; convert later to any format. | Massive data rate; expensive fast storage; needs **proxy files** for offline edit + an online conform; only high-end cameras output it. Overkill for student work. |

**LUT (Look-Up Table):** translation software that unpacks Log into your designed look (contrast, hue, exposure, saturation). Workflow: in preproduction the director sets the aesthetic with DP + art director → DP shoots Log **test footage** in representative light → DP/DIT/director try LUTs in a grading system to hit the vision → the saved LUT loads into a **field monitor** so everyone evaluates each shot against the intended final look. The LUT is **not baked in** — it only modifies the display; the flat Log source stays fully malleable in post. (Colorist Tim Stipan's Fuji-stock emulation LUT for *Leave No Trace* "inspired the filmmaking.")

### The digital camera range

| Tier | Examples | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **DSLR / mirrorless** | Canon EOS, Nikon D; Sony a7S II | Large sensors, interchangeable lenses, light/compact; but still-photo ergonomics (awkward moves/focus/monitoring), menu-buried controls, H.264 needs transcoding. |
| **Hybrid large-sensor** | Canon C300 II, Sony FS5 | Built **for motion** — 35mm/S35 sensors, interchangeable lenses, Cine + Log presets, sometimes RAW; 35mm-like palette at a fraction of the cost. |
| **Ultra-high-end (D-Cinema)** | Arri Alexa, RED Epic, Sony CineAlta, Panasonic VariCam | 4K DCI/UHD (+2K, up to 6K/8K), always RAW + simultaneous HD proxies, advanced color science, exposure latitude exceeding 35mm celluloid. **$40,000+**. |
| **Consumer** | camcorders, action cams, smartphones | Small chips (deep DoF), poor lenses, few manual controls — yet a limitation can become an aesthetic (*Tangerine*, *Unsane* on iPhone). Technology never made the filmmaker. |

## Digital sound

Address sound to the **production sound mixer** (sound recordist), who heads the field team (**boom operator**, sometimes **cable wrangler**). Decide **single-system** (audio recorded in-camera) vs. **double-system** (dedicated recorder). Serious narrative shoots go **double-system**: camera audio is inferior, and the recordist must monitor/adjust levels without intruding on the operator. Double-system means picture and sound are synced in post via the **slate/clapper board** — it identifies scene/shot/take visually and aurally and creates a one-frame sync reference (the stick snap, or timecode from a "smart slate"). Plan: single vs. double; portable mixer?; boom operator?; consistent settings (**48 kHz / 24-bit / WAV or AIFF**); mic approach per situation (boom / planted / radio lav, with wired backup); slate type; and any wild/ambient sounds to capture on location.

## Postproduction, stills, and warnings

- **Post infrastructure:** NLE software (Premiere Pro, Avid ~$300/subscription) on a powerful workstation ($3–5k) with a **RAID** (redundant disk array — a failed disk's contents are already backed up). Length, coverage volume, render-heavy VFX, and post-facility services drive schedule and budget; budget ample primary + backup storage, including for the Pro Tools mix, plus mastering, music rights, grading, and distributable copies.
- **Production stills:** depute (ideally a dedicated photographer, not a busy crew member) to shoot high-res stills — striking compositions that represent theme, character, and allure. They're vital for posters and festival/distributor packages (A24's use of Merie Wallace's *Lady Bird* stills).
- **Awful warnings — test, test, test.** Be prepared for the worst (a stolen car took a feature's only master audio → silent footage); test shoots let camera/art build the grade/LUTs in peace; check every piece of gear on arrival — never trust that a reputable supplier means it works. Murphy is waiting.
- **Production party:** an icebreaker before shooting; in an itinerant, freelance industry you re-hire compatible collaborators for life.
