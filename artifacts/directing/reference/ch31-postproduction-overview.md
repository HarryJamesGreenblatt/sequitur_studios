# Chapter 31 — Postproduction Overview

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 31.
> **Scope:** the director's-eye map of postproduction — the post team and the editor's role, the danger of the director-editor "hyphenate," the technology/workflow the director must understand, and the fourteen stages footage passes through to become the finished film. The complement, from the directing chair, to *Grammar of the Edit*.

## Core idea

*"Editing is the transformation of chance into destiny."* (Godard.) **Postproduction** is the phase where dailies metamorphose into the film the audience sees, and the editing suite is where a director learns the most about their own directing. Beginners usually cut their own work — an essential education whose rough lessons ("I need a close-up. Why didn't I shoot a damn close-up?") turn you into a director who **shoots for the edit** and welcomes collaborating with an editor. **"Editing is your second chance at directing,"** and your most important partnership will likely be with an editor who shapes the tone, rhythm, and credibility of your story. You cannot rescue a lackluster performance or a poorly conceived strategy, but film is **malleable**, and editors routinely create momentum that obscures many a passing blemish. Every director must know editing's language, creative capacity, and flexibility.

Read across the studio's own architecture, this chapter is the **Director's-eye complement to the Editor role** — the seat and vocabulary already exist ([editorial.py](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py) + [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py)); this chapter deepens *why* that seat is a separate agency from the Director and maps the ASSEMBLE→SHIP pipeline.

## The postproduction team

| Role | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Editor** | Leads the build; responsible for all practical and aesthetic cutting decisions. |
| **Assistant editors** | Organize, sync, and support; echo the editor's patience and rigor. |
| **Sound designer** | Shapes the overall sonic identity. |
| **Sound / music editor** | Cuts effects, atmospheres, and music to picture. |
| **Composer** | Writes original score. |
| **Sound-mix engineers** | Balance and master the multitrack into the final mix. |
| **Colorist** | Grades the image for tone and continuity of look. |

## The editor

The editor **answers to the director during the director's cut**, but to the **producer** if a **producer's cut** is demanded; from first assembly through final mastering, the whole team looks to the editor for leadership and focus. Building the film from production materials (picture and sound) and post materials (effects, atmospheres, music), the editor owns **all practical and aesthetic decisions**. Good editors are **patient, highly organized, endlessly experimental, and tactfully diplomatic** about getting their own way; the secluded work lets them think in deep, sustained ways — part of their value ("never trust an editor with a tan").

The editor reads the screenplay for reference but **cannot realize its design unless the footage supports it** — the job is always to make the **best possible film from the materials supplied**, which demands responsible, subjective judgment and the confidence to advocate **altering the original intentions** to serve the film's needs. Crucially, the editor was **absent from shooting**, so they bring an **unbiased, audience-like eye** to a director who often finishes exhausted, depressed, and blinkered — clinging to hard-won shots that would be better rearranged or cut. Intimate with the power of **visual juxtaposition**, the editor argues for reordering where meaning can grow, finds unglimpsed possibilities in performances, and **rebalances pacing and structure**. In the digital age this begins **during shooting** (reviewing footage, roughing scenes), and editing is never mere assembly — it's "coaxing a brilliant musical performance from imperfect, overlapping, incomplete scores." Interpretive work through picture editing and sound design includes:

- **Reworking story order**
- **Determining scene emphasis and rhythm**
- **Controlling POV and tone**
- **Increasing visual impact through color grading**

Many directors regard the alliance as so vital that a **favorite editor travels with them film to film** — Scorsese/Schoonmaker, Campion/Jenet, Coppola/Murch, Frears/Audsley, Spielberg/Kahn.

## Director-editors: the hyphenate hazard

On low-budget films the director cutting their own work is the **"hyphenate."** Without a collaborator who **wasn't on set**, and without the creative tension of a partnership, they routinely **misjudge their intentions** and fail to see the material's actuality — falling into love or hate, axing what works in disgust or clinging obsessively to **"darlings"** (folklore says you must kill them). Even accomplished directors produce some of their slackest films **wearing too many hats** (*Dances with Wolves* at 183 minutes — Costner's director's cut, 224). The film benefits from the editor's **detached, counterbalancing viewpoint as audience proxy** — steadying advocacy that yields a tougher, more balanced film than anyone can deliver alone.

## Technology, workflow, and the director

Filmmaking technology is in **accelerated evolution** — new formats, codecs, and NLE systems arrive monthly. Celluloid and its ruinous expense (and film-to-tape transfers, negative matching) are all but gone, replaced by a staggering, ever-more-complex array of digital formats. A book on directing can't cover the mindboggling options, but **knowledge is power**: you need enough familiarity to contend with editing your own work. **Research the most current information** in detailed publications, sites, and boards, and — best of all — **find someone who recently used the exact equipment, software, and workflow** you're considering, so you can profit from what they learned.

## Workflow

**Workflow** is the **stages and format path** a project takes from production to exhibition: production formats for picture and sound, editing settings, finishing formats (color grade, sound mix), the mastering format, and distribution formats. Options are many and constantly evolving, so during **preproduction** consult everyone whose job is to know current tech — cinematographer, sound recordist, editor, colorist, post sound mixer — and **settle the entire workflow before shooting**, to avoid time-consuming, expensive surprises. Lay it out across four categories:

| # | Category | Typical options |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shooting format** | Picture: HD (1080i), Log, UHD or D-Cinema (2K/4K), RAW. Audio: sample rate, bit rate, file type (WAV, AIFF). |
| 2 | **Editing format** | Picture/audio formats, frame rates, codecs, resolution; whether a **LUT** (color-correction table) is applied. |
| 3 | **Finishing & mastering** | HD (1080i), professional codec (e.g. ProRes 4:4:4), D-Cinema DCDM 2K/4K. |
| 4 | **Exhibition & distribution** | Broadcast HD (Rec. 709), Digital Cinema Package (**DCP**), Blu-ray, DVD, internet streaming, or multiple. |

**Digital Cinema (D-Cinema)** is the industry standard for digital theatrical projection, established by the **Digital Cinema Initiative (DCI)** in 2002 — essentially 2K and 4K (JPEG 2000 codec, WAV audio) — and has all but replaced 35mm projection.

> **Technical resources** (use the newest editions covering your specific formats): for **workflow**, Hurbis-Cherrier's *Voice & Vision* and Arundale/Trieu's *Modern Post*; for **NLE**, the product manual plus Kauffmann's *Avid Editing*, and Premiere/Avid web tutorials; for **color**, Van Hurkman's *Color Correction Handbook*, Hullfish's *The Art and Technique of Digital Color Correction*, and Ripple Training; for **post sound**, Avarese's *Post Sound Design*, Cross's *Audio Post Production*, and **Jay Rose's *Producing Great Sound for Film and Video***; general boards include ace-filmeditors.org, aotg.com, studentfilmmakers.com, and nofilmschool.com.

## The postproduction stages

Post is a sequence of technical and creative stages demanding close director-editor collaboration. Concentrating on those where the director holds creative or interpretive control:

1. **Transfer, log, sync, organize** footage.
2. **Review and evaluate** footage (screening dailies).
3. **Input comments; mark up** the editing script.
4. **First assembly** — edit and review.
5. **Rough cuts** with picture and primary sound.
6. **Rough cut → fine cut.**
7. **Picture lock.**
8. **Gather sound elements** — record narration, Foley, ADR, and score as needed; locate pre-recorded hard effects, ambience, music.
9. **Construct the multitrack sound design** — dialogue, ambience, effects, music.
10. **Sound mix** — EQ and mix-down of multitracks into a stereo master.
11. **Titles, graphics, and visual effects.**
12. **Color correction.**
13. **Master** the final program (at a post facility if needed).
14. **Duplicate** and distribute in multiple formats.

Together these stages are "a grueling process of transformation" — but so uplifting it's "like watching medieval alchemy turn lead into gold."

## Studio application

- **This deepens an Editor role that already exists.** The Editor seat, its ASSEMBLE-phase placement, and its owned vocabulary — `Transition`, `EditReason`, `EditCategory` — are already in [editorial.py](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py); the assembly *model* the Editor composes (the `Clip → Beat → Scene → Act → Sequence` EDL with `timeline()`/`validate()`) is [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py); and the executor that stitches rendered clips into a film is [cutter.py](../../../sequitur/cutter.py) (MoviePy). This chapter supplies the *rationale* for why that Editor is a **distinct agency** from the Director.
- **The hyphenate hazard is the codebase's founding principle.** "The director cutting their own work misjudges their intentions" is exactly why the studio keeps **agency in a component, never the container**: the Director ([director.py](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py)) *reconciles* but does not *own* the cut — the Editor does, as a separate role reading the footage with an unbiased eye. The **director's cut vs. producer's cut** authority split maps straight onto the studio's tiers: the **Director** is the agent reconciler, the **Producer** is the human (HITL) with final override, and swapping *who* sits the Editor seat is the `Judgment` seam (Heuristic **A** → Persona **B** → Human) in [judgment.py](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py).
- **The 14 stages are the ASSEMBLE→SHIP pipeline.** Stages 1–7 (log/sync → first assembly → rough → fine → picture lock) are `Phase.ASSEMBLE` work over the EDL in [edit.py](../../../sequitur/edit.py); stages 8–10 (narration, Foley/ADR, sound design, mix) are the studio's **sound layer** ([speech.py](../../../sequitur/speech.py)); stages 11–12 (titles, VFX, color) touch the image renderers ([image.py](../../../sequitur/image.py), [studio.py](../../../sequitur/studio.py)); stages 13–14 (master, distribute) are `Phase.SHIP` ([role.py](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py)). **Workflow-as-format-path** — settle every format before a frame is shot — is the studio's swappable **render-backend seam**: the EDL is deliberately renderer-free so it can serialize as a plan and hand off to any executor.
- **Cross-department through-lines.** The stages listed here are the director's-eye table of contents for *Grammar of the Edit*, which unpacks the *how* of the cut ([ch01-the-editing-process](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch01-the-editing-process.md), [ch08-editors-mindset](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch08-editors-mindset.md)); the sound stages are Rose's postproduction domain ([ch10-postproduction-workflow](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch10-postproduction-workflow.md)); and the footage arriving at stage 1 comes with the script supervisor's report from [ch30](ch30-monitoring-continuity-and-progress.md), shot under the shooting plan of [ch24](ch24-developing-the-shooting-script.md) during [ch29](ch29-directing-on-the-set.md). *(Overlap flag: this whole chapter ↔ Grammar of the Edit — the Directing chapter is the director's map of post; the Grammar chapters are the editor's working manual of the same territory.)*
