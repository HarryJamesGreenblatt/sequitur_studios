# Chapter 18 — Casting

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 18.
> **Scope:** how a director finds, tests, and chooses the human beings who will embody the characters — the search, the audition ladder, and the developmental judgment behind a cast. Grounds a **Casting** role the studio architecture does not yet model.

## Core idea

**Casting is said to be ~75% of a film's success** — the audience knows instantly whether a performance is authentic and gripping. Yet the right question is not *"Does this actor fit the father in my script?"* (which measures each hopeful against a fixed ideal and invites disappointment) but *"What kind of father would this actor **give** my film?"* Casting is therefore **developmental, not image-fulfillment**: you select for who a person can *become* under direction, not who they already are. Good casting comes from **searching far and wide, having techniques to elicit hidden potential, self-knowledge about who you should (and should not) work with, and dogged persistence** — and from generating **abundance** to choose from, handled throughout with professional courtesy.

## Who you want (and the experience trap)

Seek actors **enthusiastic about the character, loyal to the project, generous with fellow cast**, and bringing **interpretive energy that adds something to the script**. Prefer actors keen to **build their skills with you**. Directing players more experienced than yourself is a gamble: a dissenting veteran can spread disaffection until the whole cast doubts your judgment. The exception — a seasoned actor who will share expertise yet ultimately **defer to your direction** — can be fruitful for everyone.

## The audition ladder

Run auditions in the industry-standard stages; having collaborators present broadens the spectrum of reaction and dissolves the paralyzing guilt of choosing between likable, hopeful people.

| Stage | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Locate players** | **Casting call** ad → applicants submit **headshot + bio**; or an **open call** all may attend. |
| **First audition** | Screen for experiential/physical/psychological/emotional suitability; **check availability** for the shoot dates. |
| **Callbacks** | Recall the promising; test **directability**, poise under duress, on-camera presence. |
| **Final callbacks** | Sterner demands; **mix and match** for chemistry, then settle. |

Vocabulary — **sides** (selected scenes from the script), **principal casting** (main speaking parts) vs. **background casting** (clerks, diners, staff — chosen for appearance, often without audition), **casting director / agency** (paid specialists who preselect and cut labor drastically). Union note: SAG-AFTRA rules may require a **full script 24 hours before audition**. Without specialized help, substitute hard work and gumshoe ingenuity.

## Posting the call & the active search

A casting call ad describes the project in **one sentence**, gives thumbnail character descriptions (compressed enough that a reader infers appearance), and states **shooting dates** (so the unavailable self-eliminate), union stance, and remuneration (or, if none, that expenses are covered and they receive a high-res copy). Post to casting services (Backstage, Mandy, Actors Access), localized boards, theatre papers, and school billboards — *large nets catch odd fish*.

Hard-to-cast roles need an **active search**: contact theatre-group casting directors (briefly, warily — they fear poaching), producers, and film/theatre professors for names. For a child role, work through drama teachers who ask the parents to make contact — **child actors mean sustained, trust-building contact with parents at every step**. For older or unusual roles, hunt senior-citizen theatre groups and activist/neighborhood circles for individuals whose **attitude and energy overcome inexperience**.

## Non-professional actors

Cast **non-actors** when their real life mirrors the role: their lived experience brings **authenticity and a genuine "voice"** — and often new story material worth rewriting toward. A lineage runs from the Italian Neo-realists (De Sica, Rossellini) through Ray, Truffaut, Bresson (his uncorrupted "models"), Herzog (Bruno S.), Kiarostami, the Dardennes, and Loach, to Bahrani, Zeitlin, and Baker (*The Florida Project*). Assessing non-actors is **different**: no prepared monologues, no cold reads (contradictory to why you chose them). Instead:

- **Character parts** (cop, nurse, driver): find people who really do the job; gauge temperament, reliability, and whether they can simply **be** on camera as in a documentary — *not act*. Audition by semi-improvising the scene and watching them react in real professional mode.
- **Major roles**: require special personal motivation and related life experience (class, occupation, age, story) so they connect to the emotional life of the part. Discuss script situations **obliquely** ("what might you do/say here?"), rarely ask for memorized lines (they use their **own words**), then **rehearse and shoot short screen tests**. Watch for whether their real-life charisma **survives the camera** — nerves can melt it. If under duress the ego proves fragile, defensive, or self-conscious, don't cast them. Be ready to **teach the essentials of interior life** (Chs. 20–22) and to reconceive scenes around what they bring.

## Conducting the first audition

Schedule arrivals at 15–20-minute intervals into a **green room** (a holding area away from the audition space), received by a lively, understanding assistant who eases anxiety and quietly notes each actor's personality, work habits, and punctuality. Give waiting actors their **sides**; anyone without a headshot/bio completes an **audition form** (name, contacts, role, experience, references, special skills, plus director's note space). In the room: warm handshake and eye contact, introduce everyone present, make light of any camera. Ask for a **slate** (brief self-identification for the footage). Nerves aren't negative — they signal the actor cares. Shooting first auditions is useful (mandatory for later rounds); medium shots for the body, unobtrusive close-ups for the face — used for **reference more than evaluation** at this point.

## What auditions reveal

**Two brief, contrasting monologues** (the actor's own choices) expose material judgment, physical presence/rhythm/energy, **voice quality (extremely important)**, and emotional range — and whether the actor can **interpret and memorize, act with the whole body, *be* rather than act** (working outward from thought and feeling, not inward from analysis), and **find the character in themselves** rather than imitate a type. The very *choice* of material reveals self-image. **Cold readings** from your sides (read opposite a helper, after you contextualize the scene) reveal quick thinking, creativity from bare words, use of voice and body, grasp of the character's **conflict**, and intelligent questions. If it's clearly no fit, close graciously and quickly; if impressed and time allows, try a **simple adjustment** ("resentment, not apology") to test flexibility — but don't belabor it, since fatigue corrupts judgment.

## Assessment axes

Add notes to each form along these dimensions:

| Axis | Reads for |
|---|---|
| **Suitability** | age, gender, physical type, ethnicity |
| **Grasp of acting** | experience, craft knowledge |
| **Physical presence** | features, body language, movement, **voice** |
| **Innate character** | confidence, energy, sociability, attitude |
| **Directability** | interaction, flexibility, defenses, self-image |
| **Commitment** | work habits, motivation, reliability |

A dazzling audition may mean an actor merely *auditions well* yet is glib and inflexible; a shy, dedicated one may grow furthest under direction (**Brando mumbled into his shirtfront**). Match the demand to the part: a surly one-scene attendant needs no growth; a wife slowly realizing her husband is mother-dominated needs **openness, trust, and emotional reach** for a grueling process.

## Callbacks, improv, interview, mix-and-match

From callbacks onward, **always tape** with a fairly mobile, occasionally close handheld camera. Feed the actor a **specified playing**, give critical feedback and specific direction, then **run it again** — watching who *builds* on praise and fixes the flagged problems; alter the mood on a further pass to test range; observe **listening, organic reaction, and physical comfort** between paired actors. Use **improv** only if you can direct it confidently (it exposes actors; keep it low-stakes and enjoyable) — the goal is not fidelity to the scripted scene but how each **handles spontaneous interaction and incorporates direction**. In the **interview**, give the full script but tell them *not to learn lines* (which would freeze an embryonic performance); look for realism, sincerity, self-motivation, genuine interest in the drama, and the good sign of an actor excited because the script touches their own experience. Be wary of flatterers, name-droppers, the superficial, the inflexibly opinionated, the falsely enthusiastic, and anyone likely to **bail when something better comes up**. **Mix and match** to find the chemistry an audience will feel — lovers must at least *like* each other; even enemies must be responsive and interested. **Avoid any actor who starts to "direct" the others** (an insecure bid to shine brighter).

## Final choices, negative roles, and the aftermath

Cross-check final picks against physical/temperamental suitability, impact, **imprint on the part relative to the others**, speech and movement rhythms, quickness and directability, accent mimicry, **voice quality**, capacity to hold new *and* old instructions, ability to carry the character's development, project commitment, patience with **filming's slow, disjunctive progress**, ability to **re-enter an emotional state across many takes and angles**, and compatibility with cast and director. Run a **camera test** — look for actors who stay in focus and interesting *while thinking or listening*. Voice risky picks tactfully but directly; **candid confrontation reveals how an actor takes uncomfortable criticism** and paves the way for later issues.

On **negative characters and typecasting**: all acting is both a departure from self and an exploration of it, and an actor may fear you cast them for their *own* bad qualities — disastrous if believed. Probe with defense questions ("what caused this trait? what can be said for him? how does he see himself?"); the maturity of the answer guides your choice (*"there are no small parts, only small actors"*). Finally, **notify and thank everyone personally by the promised date** — offer each rejected actor something appreciative and constructive. **Filmmaking is a village**; a director who rejects sensitively is one worth approaching next time. Above all, actors crave **recognition**: liberate an actor's potential and they respond with loyalty and become your advocates — the foundation of every creative relationship being **sharing and honesty**.

- **Casting is a dimension NO current source and NO current code models** — the whole **performance layer** is new territory. In an AI-generated studio, "casting" = **defining a character's appearance and voice references**: character-consistent keyframes produced by [`ImageStudio`](../../../sequitur/image.py) and a chosen neural voice fed to [`SpeechRenderer`](../../../sequitur/speech.py). The chapter seeds a future **`Casting` role** that owns those reference artifacts the way today's roles own grammar enums.
- **A `Casting` role would follow the existing role pattern.** Model it on [`crew/role.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) (`Role`/`Department`/`Phase`/`Brief`/`Contribution`): a **plan-phase** role whose `heuristic` proposes a character's visual/vocal references and whose output the [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) reconciles. The book's assessment axes (suitability · presence · **voice** · directability) become the fields a `CharacterRef` contribution fills; the **swappable [`Judgment`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py)** seam maps cleanly — *HeuristicJudgment* ("A") picks a default look/voice, *PersonaJudgment* ("B") reasons about interpretive fit, *HumanJudgment* lets the **Producer** cast by hand.
- **"What would this actor *give* the film?" is the developmental prompt for generative casting.** Rather than matching a fixed spec, the role should generate **abundance** — several candidate look/voice references — and let the Director (and Producer HITL) choose for chemistry across the ensemble, exactly as mix-and-match tests pairwise reaction. Character consistency across shots is the generative analogue of "an actor who can re-enter the same emotional state across many takes."
- **Voice quality is the through-line to the sound layer (storyline [0011](../../../context/storyline/0011-the-voice-layer.md)).** The book flags **voice as extremely important** at every stage; casting a voice = selecting the neural voice [`SpeechRenderer`](../../../sequitur/speech.py) will drive, which in turn feeds Rose's voice-over and dialogue-editing craft ([Producing Great Sound Ch. 9](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch09-the-voice-over.md), [Ch. 13](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch13-editing-voices.md)) and the Taxonomy's [Voice](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch05-voice.md) axis.
