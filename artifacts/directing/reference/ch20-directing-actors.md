# Chapter 20 — Directing Actors

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 20.
> **Scope:** the director's working method with performers — building trust, giving actable direction, diagnosing the standard performance problems, and knowing how much to rehearse. Grounds the **Director working with the performance layer**.

## Core idea

Unlike the crew, **actors have no equipment to stand behind and nowhere to hide** — their work is intense, exposed, and makes them vulnerable, easily discouraged, and quick to compare themselves unfavorably. They seldom lack challenge or drive; what they need is **recognition**, and the director is their **sole audience** — all direction and all approval flow from you alone. **Good directing is good leadership.** The whole craft reduces to helping actors cast off self-consciousness so they can simply **be**, and giving **short, practical, actable** direction — never asking for feelings or effects, which actors cannot summon.

## The director–actor relationship

- **Make contact.** Human contact matters most; actors' handshakes and greetings are a rite of reassurance, not sexuality. Respond warmly in kind — while remaining an authority figure who treats **all equally, without condescension**.
- **Protect the channel.** Everyone on the unit (and every visitor) must be briefed **never** to comment on an actor's work; no actor may critique another. Any suggestion, however useful, is **routed confidentially through you** to decide whether it goes further.
- **Build trust and authority.** Know the script and characters thoroughly; be **accessible** and don't bury yourself at the camera (nothing dispirits actors more than a director hovering with the crew); **direct inductively** by asking questions and truly listening (*what people discover for themselves they never forget*); don't waste their time; expect to be **tested**, especially when you revise earlier instructions — each actor, carrying only one character, will catch things you missed, so learning is two-way.
- **Direct positively and equably.** Feedback is so vital that most actors feel **under-recognized**; the larger the cast, the truer this is. Reframe every note as **development, not censure** — "keep the intensity, but get her attention by becoming dangerously quiet," not "less wild." Prefer "I wonder if you could try…"; say as little as needed; **never intellectualize or think aloud** (it clogs the actor's mind). Never favor anyone, never humiliate anyone before colleagues, never get romantically involved with the cast. **Thank people at every session's end**, spinning even a poor day optimistically — *people work best for those who expect well of them*. Start with a **small cast** and re-use your best relationships.

## The common problems — diagnosis & cure

The recurring failure is the actor who, under pressure, **acts from the head** — signaling ideas and **indicating** because he's lost (or never found) the character's heart and mind. Your job is to spot and dismantle the **log-jams of tension** so the flow of mental/emotional focus resumes. Sometimes the fault is the **script or blocking**, not the actor — a reworded line or freer movement clears it.

| Problem | What it looks like | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| **Lost focus & relaxation** | conviction drains from the lines, tension shows (shoulders, hands, voice — varies per person) | there's always a reason; find where each actor carries tension, lower anxiety by redirecting attention or recalling something they just did well; note the recurring failure point without stopping — usually a **doubted line** |
| **Missing mind–body connection** | you're not convinced by the bodily action/tone | stay relaxed enough to react as an audience would; run an internal **barometer** of highs and lows, name the **high points** first, then by subtraction hand the problem back — nearly always **fuzzy objectives** |
| **Anticipating / not adapting** | actor pre-reacts or lags; reaction feels calculated and dead | make him articulate line-by-line what others are **implying** and work off those clues; cure with an **interior monologue** built on moment-by-moment signals |
| **Acting in isolation** | player insulated in preconceptions, not feeding or receiving from the cast | private talk; let go, live in the moment, trust the text; a playful **improv** (only if you can direct it) then back to the scene, feeding small intentions and **deliberating before reacting** — reveals **communion** |
| **Missing interior life** | lines without conviction, timing "all over the map" (esp. untrained) | teach the silent **interior monologue**; for a stumbling two-hander, have each improvise the character's thoughts aloud before and after every line |
| **Missing subtext** | moments not aimed at anyone | make each actor state what the character is trying to **get/do/accomplish**, to whom, and whether met; sharpen with a **first-person active verb + target + intended effect**; language matters — "**make** my manager **admit** who **stole**" beats "ask… tell… took"; **raise the stakes** |
| **Generalized interpretation** | "acting in general" — all traits at once, a fuzzy color-wash | acting is **one clear intention/action/demand after another**; break the action into steps, or have the character's interior monologue spoken low aloud |
| **Distancing & indicating** | too much voice/action/emotion; actor grades himself, severed from character (root cause: **intellectualizing**, often from over-analysis) | ask for an interior monologue; have the character **withhold** feelings (interesting work, more tension); **insist on personalizing** — convert any "she/her" to first person, any passive voice to active |

## Intensity, intimacy, and the actor's sphere

Interactions must be **contained and purposeful, not florid and "actory"** — unmotivated pacing and flailing signal trouble. In cinema, characters gain power when gesture is **functional and minimal** (Michael Caine on overcoming the impulse to blink). Meaningful action is **purposeful, minimal, and small in compass** — to a distant onlooker "nothing is happening," yet on the big screen the scene is electric, an **intimate bubble** the camera captures completely (even an IMAX lens is a perfect voyeur, catching the tightening of a jaw). Reassure theatre-trained actors: if they're **inside the character**, the camera does the rest. Redirect the over-large by shrinking the world — *"a bubble of space just big enough for you and your partner; talk only to him; there's no one else, no camera."* The magnified voices stop and intensity climbs.

## Stubborn artificiality

An actor whose idea of acting comes from **TV commercials** hams and projects an *idea* of the part. Take her aside, get her talking through the character's **interior monologue**, and have her **model on someone she knows** — a strong way to anchor a part. Busy maintaining inner processes, she can no longer stand outside and watch herself act (the root of the problem). As a last resort, **clandestinely tape** the unnatural acting and show it beside grabbed footage of the actor in normal conversation — the shock can open them to coaching. With an **incurable** voice-projector, the honest solution may be to **recast** — though this frightens the rest of the cast.

## How much to rehearse — and not over-directing

Actors fear **over-rehearsal** (drilling to a master plan); learn to recognize when they've **"nailed it"** — truly inhabiting the character and the moment — and refresh a tiring cast by moving on and returning. **Never rehearse without plans and objectives** (the cast will sense wasted time). Decide which scenes are **pivotal**; rehearsal that digs for meaning and cross-script resonance also **habituates actors to improvising**, so on-set changes don't faze them.

**Don't over-direct** — rehearsing past improvement, or micromanaging, usually signals a director who **distrusts instinct and over-intellectualizes**. Many moments are just simple: *getting into a car is getting into a car*. And some scenes are so **delicate** that heavy rehearsal drains spontaneity — work with actors **individually** and save the **first ensemble performance for the camera** (Sarah Polley deliberately left the seduction scene in *Take This Waltz* unrehearsed).

## The element of surprise

Because genuine surprise is nearly impossible to *act* once the actor knows it's coming (Ken Loach: it becomes "a technical matter of timing rather than an instinctive reaction"), some directors **withhold information**: release script sides only a day or two out, hold back plot details until the scene plays, shoot chronologically, or change a scene for one actor without alerting the other. Loach withheld the true outcome from Martin Compston in *Sweet Sixteen* — the actor believed he was about to "kill" a man; the on-camera **non-comprehension** when he's jumped and stopped is real. Powerful for actors who **anticipate**, but not repeatable — actors catch on fast.

## Some dos and don'ts

- **Set limited, positive goals** — "see if you can open the door softly," not "don't make a racket."
- **Direct attention to a specific action** in a specific moment — "figure out what he meant as you turn away" (generalized notes don't help).
- **Suggest a different subtext** — "close the door on him with finality rather than regret."
- **Remind actors where the character just came from** — films shoot in small, out-of-order increments; actors need constant orientation.
- **Remind actors nobody is present** — ignore the crew, never look at the camera.
- **Never demonstrate** how to play it (you'd get a copy of yourself; what you want is unique to that actor).
- **Never give line readings** (reading the line with the desired inflection is insulting and reveals a lack of imagination).
- **Never say "just be yourself"** (it sets off "which me does he want?"); focus the actor on the **character's experience** instead.
- **Never ask for something "smaller"** (taken as a barb); ask for the **same intensity with more intimacy**.

## Studio application

- **This is where the Director meets the performance layer that NO current code models.** The [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) already **reconciles** crew contributions; this chapter extends that seat to a new, unmodeled department — **coaching the actor/performance concept** (Ch. 19) rather than the camera/lighting/grip grammar the [`crew/`](../../../sequitur/crew/) roles own today. In a generative studio the "problem" table becomes **diagnostic passes over a take**: fuzzy objective → sharpen the performance record; distancing → re-anchor intent — the render-side analogue of re-briefing and re-generating.
- **"Actable, not effect" is the prompt-engineering law for the render backends.** The chapter's core rule — *give playable actions, never ask for feelings or results* — is exactly how intent should reach [`prompt.py`](../../../sequitur/prompt.py) and the video/voice renderers: encode **verb → target → outcome** and **business** (Ch. 19), not adjectives like "look contemptuous." "**Raise the stakes**" and "**suggest a different subtext**" are concrete, structured re-generation levers a *PersonaJudgment* could apply.
- **The barometer and the redo loop map onto the Producer's HITL judgment.** "Name the high points, then by subtraction address the low" is a review protocol for the **HeuristicJudgment → PersonaJudgment → HumanJudgment** seam ([`crew/judgment.py`](../../../sequitur/crew/judgment.py)): the Director/Judgment proposes, the **Producer** (the human) confirms what "rings true," and unusable takes are re-driven — the render analogue of another rehearsal pass. Withholding/surprise and "save the first performance for the camera" translate to **generation strategy** (how much intent to fix vs. leave open) — decisions a future performance role would carry in its [`Brief`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py).
- **Cross-department through-lines.** *Intensity in a small compass* is a **shot-scale** decision shared with the [Grammar of the Shot](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/ch01-the-shots.md) close-up and [composition](../../grammar%20of%20the%20shot/reference/ch02-shot-composition.md) chapters — the tighter the framing, the smaller the performance the render must carry. Subtext and withheld feeling ride the **voice layer** ([storyline 0011](../../../context/storyline/0011-the-voice-layer.md), [`speech.py`](../../../sequitur/speech.py)) and Rose's [voice-editing](../../producing%20great%20sound%20for%20film%20and%20video/reference/ch13-editing-voices.md) craft; the whole interior-life problem is the [Point of View](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch07-point-of-view.md) layer made visible on screen.
