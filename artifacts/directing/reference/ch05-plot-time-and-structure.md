# Chapter 5 — Plot, Time, and Structure

> Abridged from Michael Rabiger & Mick Hurbis-Cherrier, *Directing: Film Techniques and Aesthetics* (6th ed.), Ch. 5.
> **Scope:** the design of the story's vessel — **plot** (the ordering of events), the **handling of time**, the choice of **structure**, and the **premise/theme** yardsticks that keep it unified. Also the special economy of the **short film**.

## Core idea

Every film has an **optimal structure** — the simplest vessel that best conveys its dramatic issues and their outcome. As with all design, *less is more*. You begin by deciding how to sequence events and how to handle time, and from those two decisions the structure emerges. **Plot** is the order in which events are told; it is *means, not substance* — a poor plot can kill a good story, but no plot mechanics can rescue a poor one. What audiences actually connect to is character, conflict, and the meaning that emerges. So plot must serve, above all, to build tension and generate the **dramatic questions** that keep viewers asking *"And now what?"*

## What is plot?

- **Story** = the overall chronological progression of the complete work.
- **Dramatic narrative** = how that story is told — what is selected, emphasized, represented, and (above all) in what **order**.
- **Plot** = that selection-and-ordering of dramatic moments. Its best form is dictated by the nature of the story and the storyteller's intentions. Each incident must stand in **logical, meaningful relationship** to what came before and lead with **seeming inevitability** to what follows.

Plot is "the rules of the universe against which the characters struggle" (Roemer) — which is why plot talk always concerns what's *likely* and what we anticipate next. Stories show only fractions of a life; the writer selects significant incidents to stress one particular angle, implying a whole world beyond the frame. Delivery matters as much as content: *"Why did the chicken cross the road?"* lands; the same fact stated flat does not.

## Cause and effect

The degree of plotting depends on the story's essence:

- **Tight cause-and-effect** — scene A causes B causes C. Action and mystery films need this to guarantee **forward momentum** and clear motivation. (*The King's Speech*'s opening six beats are a tight chain.)
- **Looser logic** — character- or theme-driven stories may admit chance, ambiguity, randomness, irony, or a quirky narrator "voice." These are *never truly random*; they cohere by a logic other than linear cause and effect (emotional, psychological).
- *The Messenger* keeps a linear chronology but **reverses cause and effect for motivation** — showing consequence first, revealing cause late, per Bresson: *"Let the cause follow the effect, not accompany it or precede it."* Withholding the cause is what generates its dramatic questions.

## Flow and inevitability

A well-crafted plot flows with an **exhilarating sense of inevitability** because it contains nothing gratuitous, facile, or unlikely. Respecting the logic of characters and situations generates **forward momentum** — the thing screenwriters work hardest to achieve, and which directors must understand before transforming a script into a film.

## Character-, plot-, and theme-driven stories

Locating the *source* of momentum answers "what kind of film is this?"

| Drive | Momentum comes from | Character change | Example |
|-------|---------------------|------------------|---------|
| **Character-driven** | the protagonist's desires, decisions, reactions | central — reveal/transform the person | *The Hurt Locker* (war *changes the man*, not a battle won) |
| **Plot-driven** | the pattern of external forces | minimal — the character is a constant | *Raiders of the Lost Ark* (Indie takes what the plot throws, never morally wavers) |
| **Theme-driven** | ideas moving the film moment to moment | serves the argument | (see *Premise and Theme*) |

Most films are a **mélange**; what matters is knowing where the essential source lives, and why material is generated. In character-driven work the protagonist becomes a **surrogate for the author's voice**.

## Organizing time

Cinema has enormous **temporal elasticity** — it can suspend, reverse, expand, contract, or repeat time. Design begins with two questions: **how much time does the story cover** (a few hours in *Tangerine*; three months in *The Messenger*; 14 years in *The King's Speech*; three generations in *Sunshine* — all ~two hours long), and **how to condense** it into screen duration (3–30 min for a short, ~70–120 for a feature). Almost every film therefore presents *only* story-crucial events and excludes whatever is extraneous or inferable.

## Where to begin and end

- **Beginning is a contract with the audience** — it announces what the film is about. Start where the conflict starts. *Tangerine* opens the day Sin-Dee learns of the cheating; *The King's Speech* opens on the disastrous first broadcast; **backstory** emerges only as needed, through dialogue rather than dedicated scenes.
- **Ending** is chosen just as deliberately — at the point the objective resolves and the dramatic questions are answered. Opening on a failed speech and closing on a triumphant one traces a satisfying arc *and* a lovely symmetry.

## Options for organizing time

From strictly linear to freely fluid:

| Strategy | Definition | Exemplars |
|----------|------------|-----------|
| **Real time** | no ellipses; story duration = running time | *Cléo from 5 to 7*; short *Nine Lives* episodes |
| **Chronological** | events in order of occurrence, usually **elliptical** ("life with the dull bits cut out") | *The Messenger*, *The King's Speech*; *4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days*; a stray flashback (*Fruitvale Station*) |
| **In medias res** | open at a crisis as a hook, then jump back and run forward to it | *Iron Man*, *Into the Wild* — puts the audience *ahead* of the protagonist |
| **Frame narrative** | a framing device (often a narrator) makes the central story an extended flashback | *Caligari*, *Slumdog Millionaire*; *The Social Network* (two deposition frames → multi-POV, bridges gaps) |
| **Parallel time** | intercut multiple storylines of comparable weight for thematic association | Griffith's *Intolerance*; *The Hours*; *Dunkirk* (three threads spanning a week / two days / one hour) |
| **Subjective time** | chronology rearranged to mirror a character's recall; intruding flashbacks | *Hiroshima Mon Amour*, *Beginners*, *Distant Voices, Still Lives* |
| **Repeated time** | replay a moment for new perspective/context/meaning | *Groundhog Day*, *Run Lola Run*, *Rashomon* (four tellings; also a frame story) |
| **Reverse chronology** | events ordered backward in time | *Two Friends*; mixed with forward time in *Eternal Sunshine*, *Memento* |

## Structure

**Structure** is the overall design shaping the plot's flow — the broader logic of the narrative. The **three-act structure** (introductory Act I / development Act II / resolution Act III) is powerful and effective, but cinema is **vastly more malleable**; countless great films break the "rules" to serve their story:

- **Episodic** — *Easy Rider* (loosely connected road incidents, each a facet of the same inescapable conflict).
- **Cyclical / poetic** — *Le Quattro Volte* (four cycles of transmuting life energy, no dialogue).
- **Episodic-triptych** — *Moonlight* (childhood / adolescence / adulthood — three-act-*like* but temporally disconnected chapters).
- **Classic three-act done perfectly** — *The Artist* (genre-appropriate homage).
- **Fractured / collage** — *I'm Not There* (Dylan across six actors; Haynes chose "refraction, not condensation" because the subject defied tidy structure).

Scholars (Kristin Thompson, Robert McKee) posit four-, five-, six-, and eight-act schemes — but these are largely *after-the-fact* analyses. The working advice (Paul Thompson): *"Read all the books, learn it all, then use whatever is helpful for your own story."* **Work organically from the inside — let content and intention suggest structure, not the reverse.** An iconoclast's biopic shouldn't be forced into a tidy arc; a lean crime thriller may fit three acts perfectly. But you *must* begin developing structure from the outset — proceeding without it is like driving blind through a strange city.

## Premise and theme

What holds all the dramatic activity together is the **concept** and the **themes**:

- **Premise (concept)** = the *heart* — the overarching idea informing every scene, focused into a sharp sentence or two (main characters + primary conflict).
- **Theme** = the *soul* — the underlying idea, message, or moral that illuminates the human experience. Themes are **rarely stated overtly**; they emerge from the chemistry of narrative elements and directorial approach. A film may have one clear theme or several irreducible ones.

Premise and theme are **developmental yardsticks**: test every scene, acting choice, and staging idea against them; whatever fails to reflect them is probably extraneous. (*The King's Speech*: premise = Albert must submit to an Australian commoner's unorthodox therapy to master his stammer and become an effective king; themes = friendship/trust overcoming frailty, and true friendship transcending class — sealed by Lionel's final cue, *"Say it to me, as a friend."*)

## Short films and story scale

Every dramatic principle applies to shorts, with extra freedom and a stricter economy. A feature is a novella; **a short is a poem or song** — deft characterization, highly compressed narrative, a fresh focused POV, where every element carries more than one function:

- Shorts are **not** feature ideas crammed small.
- One central idea, a quickly recognizable situation — no room for multiple layers or historical context.
- Usually **one main character** and a **single sharp turning point**; development results from one event.
- Conflicts and actions are **self-evident** — explication and backstory become unnecessary.
- Locations, imagery, and action must be **vividly evocative** of character *and* theme.
- Familiar **genre elements** serve as storytelling shorthand.

### Short films and flexibility

Two advantages: shorts needn't sustain tension over a long span, and audiences *expect* a short to push the envelope. So a short can be built around a **non-goal-oriented dramatic question**, replacing "will the protagonist get what they're after?" with, e.g.: mysterious/ambiguous activity explained at the end · a revealing slice-of-life portrait · a discovery that shifts perception · an experience that reveals true nature · a puzzle to solve · an allegorical journey · a **setup/pay-off** (ironic twist, epiphany, punch line, perception shift). *Nine Lives*' "Maggie" runs a **setup/perception-shift**: commonplace cemetery-picnic activity, a few incongruous details, then the reveal that this is her *daughter's* grave and the girl was conjured — recasting everything retroactively. At 14 minutes it works beautifully; a feature could not sustain an hour of conflict-free activity before its revelation.

## Studio application

- **This chapter grounds the plan-phase design of the edit [`Sequence`](../../../sequitur/edit.py).** Plot (event *ordering*) and time-organization are precisely the decisions that fix the macro shape a post-phase [Editor](../../../sequitur/crew/editorial.py) assembles into `Act`s and a `Sequence`. The eight time strategies form a **closed vocabulary** a future `Screenwriter` role could own — the plan-phase counterpart to the Editor's [`Transition`/`EditCategory`](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch06-transitions-and-edit-categories.md) enums.
- **Premise/theme are the `Brief`-level invariants and the studio's `validate()` criterion.** "Test every scene against premise and theme; cut what fails" is directly the discipline the crew engine needs: a [`Director`](../../../sequitur/crew/director.py) reconciling contributions, and a [`Sequence.validate()`](../../../sequitur/edit.py), should reject any beat that serves neither. Premise ≈ the story-layer form of a [`Brief`](../../../sequitur/crew/role.py) premise/mood field.
- **"Character- vs plot- vs theme-driven"** is a routing signal for the whole crew: a character-driven brief allocates coverage and edit rhythm to *interiority* (favoring the [POV](../../the%20screenwriter%27s%20taxonomy/reference/ch07-point-of-view.md) protagonist), a plot-driven brief to *external situation and set-pieces*. This is the dramaturgical twin of the Taxonomy's Pathway divergence flags.
- **Cross-department through-line:** *cause-and-effect* / *flow and inevitability* is the story-layer statement of the same "every cut needs a reason and must feel motivated" rule the Editor enforces at the micro scale ([Grammar of the Edit Ch. 5](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch05-when-to-cut.md), [Ch. 8](../../grammar%20of%20the%20edit/reference/ch08-editors-mindset.md)) — plot sets the macro logic, the cut executes the micro.
